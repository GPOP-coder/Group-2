## The Core Idea

A business has to make sense in order for people to commit to it. That single sentence is the foundation of Strategic Logics.

When people don't understand what a business is doing — its products, its direction, what it's trying to accomplish — they don't commit. They go through the motions. They guess. They work at cross-purposes with the person next to them.

Strategic Logics is a facilitated executive discussion process designed to build unified strategic understanding across a leadership team. It is not a strategic plan. It is the structured conversation from which a strategic plan can be built — and from which an entire organization can align behind a common direction.

> "The whole purpose is to ensure that everybody in the organization gets it. That they understand where we are going, what we are going to do — and that they step forward together as a team in accomplishing their strategic intentions."

The process works by walking an executive team through nine elements of their business in a specific, logical order. Each element builds on the one before it. Skip one, or take them out of order, and the logic breaks down.

---

## Who Does It Need to Make Sense To?

Before working through the nine logics, it's worth asking: who does the business need to make sense to?

- **Customers** — if they don't understand the concept, they don't buy
- **Employees** — if it doesn't make sense to them, they won't commit to it
- **Investors and lenders** — if they don't get it, they don't fund it
- **Managers and executives** — if they're not unified around it, neither is anyone else

It is remarkable how often executive teams are not in agreement about what their own business is doing or where it's going. Strategic Logics surfaces that misalignment quickly — and creates the structured space to resolve it.

## The Nine Logics — Why Order Matters

The nine logics must be worked in sequence. They build on each other. If you jump ahead — if you start with economic logic (how much money do we need to make?) before you understand your product, your customers, and your market — you are backing into strategy from a number, which is among the most common and costly mistakes executive teams make.

**The sequence:**

1. Product Logic
2. Customer Logic
3. Competitor Logic
4. Marketing Logic
5. Economic Logic
6. Quality and Service Logic
7. Resources and Technology Logic
8. Organizational Logic
9. Human Resource Logic

**A common objection:** "shouldn't the customer come first?" Many books say so. The customer-first philosophy sounds right. But if the customer truly came first, then whatever the customer does is right — and anyone who has managed a casino floor knows that is not always true.

**Another common objection:** "quality is job one." During the 2008 recession, Ford made quality trucks. But people weren't buying trucks — they wanted cheap transportation. Quality was irrelevant to the purchase decision. The product logic hadn't changed, but the economic reality of the customer had.

Service, quality, organizational structure — all of these are conclusions that follow from understanding the first four logics. They are not starting points.

## The Nine Logics in Detail

### 1. Product Logic

Every business started with a product. The founder had a skill, an idea, something they knew how to do or make. Bill Gates wasn't sitting in his garage asking what customers wanted — he was playing with computers because he loved computers. The product came first.

For businesses that already exist — which is most of the clients a consultant will work with — the product logic question is: what exactly are we selling, and what business are we actually in?

In the casino industry, this question alone can take a day. Are we in the gaming business? The entertainment business? The resort business? The convention business? The retail business? Each answer produces a completely different set of downstream strategic conclusions.

The product logic discussion should produce a clear, unified answer to: what are we selling, and what business are we in?

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 2. Customer Logic

Once you know what your product is, the question becomes: who wants it? A customer is only someone who wants your product. If they don't want it, they are not your customer.

This discussion has two sides that both matter:

- **Who are we going after?** Who is the high-probability customer for our product?
- **Who are we NOT going after?** Which customers have so little value to us that we should stop spending time, marketing dollars, and attention on them?

Many businesses spend enormous resources pursuing customers who have little value to them. The customer logic discussion makes that visible and forces a deliberate choice.

The question "which customers should we kill off?" sounds harsh. It is also one of the most productive discussions a leadership team can have.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 3. Competitor Logic

Your competitors are only those organizations going after your customers with a similar product. Not everyone in the same industry. Not everyone with similar physical assets. The competitive set is defined by product AND customer alignment.

A high-end Strip casino is not competing with a downtown Las Vegas budget property — even though both have slot machines and hotel rooms. Different products, different customers, different competitive set.

**The two sub-strategies of competitor logic:**

- **Attack** — actively take customers, market share, and positioning from competitors
- **Defend** — protect existing customers and market share from competitor incursion

Defending is expensive and reactive. Attacking is energizing. A well-facilitated competitor discussion gets executives excited about the offensive opportunities they've been leaving on the table.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 4. Marketing Logic

Once you know your product, your customers, and your competitors, you can market intelligently. You market to your customers, you market your product, and you position your product against your competitors.

The two sub-strategies of marketing logic are what Mac called **"sucking and sticking."**

**Sucking Strategies**

If your customers are not at your business, where are they? Wherever they are, your job is to get them here. Sucking strategies are everything you do to draw customers in — advertising, promotions, word of mouth, your physical presence on a highway, your digital presence online. You suck them off the couch, out of the competition, in through the door.

**Sticking Strategies**

Once they're here, how long do they stay? How much do they spend? Do they come back? Sticking strategies build loyalty, extend the visit, deepen the relationship. IKEA's winding path through the store. The milk at the back of the grocery store. The host who makes a guest want to return to their section specifically.

Every employee is part of both strategies. The server who makes a guest feel genuinely welcomed is a sucking strategy (word of mouth, reputation) and a sticking strategy (the guest stays longer, returns more often) simultaneously.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 5. Economic Logic

Economic logic is where boards almost always start. They hand the organization a number — "we need $100 million in EBITDA this year" — and everyone backs into it. This is backwards.

You cannot know how much money you will make until you understand:

- The state of your product (renovation needs, quality gaps, investment required)
- The behavior of your customers (economic conditions, demand shifts, COVID, recession)
- What your competitors are doing (attacking you means defensive spending; market opportunity means offensive investment)
- Your marketing approach (what it costs to suck and stick)

Economic logic is the output of the first four logics, not a starting point handed down from above.

The economic logic discussion covers: revenue enhancement strategies, cost containment, pricing, expansion plans, mergers and acquisitions, and how market conditions translate into financial realities.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 6. Quality and Service Logic

Now — and only now — do you talk about quality and service. Because quality and service standards are a function of your product and your customers, not a philosophical aspiration.

Five-star hotel, five-star customers: you need five-star quality and five-star service. That costs significant money and should be reflected in your economic logic.

Budget hotel, budget customers: you can operate at a lower quality level and they will be satisfied. Attempting to provide five-star service to a customer who paid for a two-star experience is waste — and may actually make that customer uncomfortable.

Is it possible for quality to be too high? Yes. A hotel that dramatically upgrades its product may find it has displaced its own customer base — the guests it had no longer feel comfortable, and the guests it wants haven't arrived yet.

**The tattoo policy example:** whether an organization allows visible tattoos on employees is not a bias question, a values question, or an HR preference. It is a logical conclusion. Five-star product, five-star customers — what does your appearance policy look like? Young, hip brand going after young, hip customers — what does your appearance policy look like? The logic determines the answer.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 7. Resources and Technology Logic

Given your quality and service requirements, how do you deliver them? Do you use people, technology, or some combination?

Technology is faster than people — that is its purpose. If your technology is slower than your people, you have bad technology.

Older customers often prefer human interaction. Younger customers increasingly prefer technology and resist waiting for a person. COVID accelerated technology adoption across age groups in ways that have permanently changed the calculus.

The resources and technology decision also includes physical resources: equipment, facilities, tools. Sometimes getting the quality standard right means better kitchen equipment, not more kitchen staff.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 8. Organizational Logic

Organization logic is number eight out of nine. It is astonishing how much executive time gets spent on org charts, restructuring, and divisional realignment when the question of what the organization should look like can't be answered until you know everything that precedes it.

If you're moving toward technology-heavy service delivery, you need more IT staff and fewer front-line service staff. If you're pursuing the convention business, your organizational structure looks different than if you're a gaming-primary property. The structure follows the strategy — not the other way around.

A company once sent an RFP asking Mac to come in and analyze how to reorganize. He asked: what does the future you're organizing toward look like? They didn't know. He couldn't help them restructure until they could answer that question.

Form follows function. You can't determine the form until you know the function.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

---

### 9. Human Resource Logic

Human resource professionals hate being last. But the logic is airtight: how do you know what kind of people you need, how to pay them, how to train them, how to manage their appearance, or what your hiring standards should be — until you know everything else?

Five-star product, five-star customers: you need five-star employees, pay them accordingly, train them extensively, hold them to a five-star appearance standard.

Budget product, budget customers: you can hire and pay accordingly, simplify training, relax the appearance standard.

The human resource logic discussion covers approximately 28 distinct elements, including:

- Compensation philosophy (pay to attract and retain talent, vs. market midpoint, vs. cost-minimization)
- Appearance and grooming standards (derived from product and customer, not personal bias)
- Hiring standards and profiles (match to the customer being served)
- Training investment (service training, quality training, technical skills)
- Succession planning and development
- Employee philosophy (people as high-value assets vs. interchangeable resources)

The employee philosophy discussion often reveals the deepest philosophical disagreements in the room — and those disagreements, if unresolved, will undermine every HR policy, procedure, process, and practice the organization tries to implement.

**Key question:** How will we measure success of this strategic intention?

## Philosophies Drive Strategies

Behind every conflict in an executive team, there is a philosophical disagreement. They may be arguing about tactics — whether to use television advertising, whether to allow tattoos, whether to expand — but the real culprit is that they were never philosophically aligned in the first place.

**Philosophies drive strategies. Strategies drive tactics.** If you are not philosophically aligned, you will never be strategically aligned. If you are not strategically aligned, you will never be tactically aligned.

The facilitated discussion surfaces these philosophical disagreements. When they appear, the right move is to stop — stop talking about tactics, stop talking about strategies — and go back to the philosophy. What do we believe about this? What are the implications of each belief? What can we actually agree to?

Once philosophical agreements are reached, they should be written down, codified, and anchored. New executives and employees should be oriented to them. The agreements don't change unless the world changes — and even then, the change in philosophy should be a deliberate decision, not drift.

### A Story That Illustrates the Point

Mac was facilitating a strategic logics session with a casino owned by one man. During the marketing logic discussion, the executive team enthusiastically aligned around being good corporate citizens — community involvement, sponsoring local events, encouraging employees to volunteer.

The owner listened, then erupted. He wasn't interested in the community. It brought him no revenue. He was not going to spend money on it. End of discussion.

Three key executives left within 30 days. Not because of the decision itself, but because they realized they were not in philosophical alignment with the owner of the business. They couldn't see it before the session. The session made it visible.

That is the value of surfacing philosophical agreements explicitly. Sometimes the result is alignment. Sometimes the result is clarity about misalignment that was always there but was never named.

## Using This Framework as a Facilitator

Strategic Logics is a facilitated discussion, not a lecture or a consulting deliverable. The facilitator's role is to ask questions, follow the logic wherever the group takes it, surface disagreements when they emerge, and anchor agreements in writing as they are reached.

### What You're Listening For

- Philosophical disagreements hidden inside tactical arguments
- Executives who are not in agreement about what business they are in
- Customers the organization is pursuing who have little value to it
- Competitors who are misidentified (too broad, too narrow)
- Assumptions about quality and service that were never derived from product and customer logic

### What the Discussion Produces

- **Unified vision of the future** — the executive group in genuine agreement about where they are going
- **Strategic intentions** — clear statements of what we intend to do (and not do) in each logic
- **Philosophical agreements** — written, codified, anchored statements of who we are and what we believe
- **Focus on what matters most** — not everything on the list matters equally; the discussion reveals what does
- **Foundation for a strategic plan** — the discussion is not the plan, but it makes the plan possible

### Timing

A company that knows who it is and where it wants to go: two days. A company that doesn't: three. One client took nine days — not because Mac moved slowly, but because the leadership team refused to move off each logic until they reached genuine agreement. That is the right instinct. Nine days of real alignment is worth more than two days of false consensus.

### The Measure Anchoring Every Logic

Each logic ends with the same question: **How will we measure success of this strategic intention?** This prevents the discussion from remaining abstract. Every strategic intention must have a measurable outcome attached to it. If you cannot measure it, you cannot manage it, and you cannot hold anyone accountable to it.

---

## What This Framework Is For

Strategic Logics is the starting point for every engagement with an executive leadership team. Before labor standards, before workforce analytics, before any operational recommendation — a business has to make sense. To its customers, its employees, its investors, and its own leadership.

When people understand the why — why the product is what it is, why we pursue these customers and not those ones, why the quality standard is set where it is, why the organization looks the way it does — they commit. They trust the direction. They defend it upward. They step forward together.

That is what this is all about.
