## 1. How Pete Runs an Engagement

### The Philosophy
PWS engagements are not implementations. They are consulting relationships. The difference:
- An implementation delivers a configured system
- A consulting relationship delivers a working operation

Pete's clients call because something is broken, underperforming, or never worked right in the first place. They are not buying software configuration. They are buying judgment, diagnosis, and a path forward.

### The Entry Point
Most PWS engagements begin one of three ways:
1. **Post-implementation failure** — the system was implemented but never worked correctly
2. **Performance degradation** — the system worked, then stopped working (staffing changes, operational changes, standards drift)
3. **Referral from within the Unifocus network** — CSM, consultant, or client who knows Pete's work

## 3. The First Conversation

### What Pete Listens For
- What is actually broken vs. what the client thinks is broken
- Whether there is a champion onsite who will carry the work forward
- What the history of the implementation is
- Whether management understands the why or just wants the problem to go away
- What their relationship with their Unifocus CSM looks like

### What Pete Does Not Do
- Does not promise a specific outcome before seeing the data
- Does not let the client define the scope based on what they think they need
- Does not accept "just fix the schedule" as a complete brief

### The Reframe
Most clients come in with a symptom. Pete's job in the first conversation is to understand whether the symptom is the problem or a signal of a deeper problem.

## 4. Onsite Observation Methodology

### Why Observation Is Irreplaceable
Data tells you what happened. Observation tells you why.

A client can have years of labor data, and it will not tell you that:
- Room attendants are going to a different floor to get supplies because their floor's closet is empty
- Half a par of supplies is sitting in hallway boxes because someone thought rationing saves money
- Stained laundry is being put back into circulation
- High dusters are inconsistently used
- The closet is disorganized in a way that costs 3 minutes per room

These things cannot be seen in a report. They can only be seen by being there.

### The Observation Process

**Day 1 — Build Rapport**
Do not time anyone on Day 1. Do not take visible notes. Introduce yourself, explain why you're there, and be genuinely curious. Ask open-ended questions. Have lunch with the team if possible.

People will perform differently when observed. The first half-day, they are performing. By the end of Day 1, they start to forget you're watching.

**Day 2+ — Real Observation**
By Day 2, guards are down. You start seeing what actually happens:
- How many times does the attendant go back to the cart?
- What do they bring into the room each trip?
- What do they leave behind?
- Where do they go when they need something that isn't on their floor?
- What is their actual sequence in the room?

**Minimum Time for Valid Observation**
- At minimum: one full day (see a full shift start to finish)
- Ideal: a full week (see a busy day and a slow day, high checkout and low checkout)
- For a resort or complex property: longer

### Pete's Observation App
Pete built a mobile app to record and time observations systematically. Key functions:
- Log each observation with timestamp
- Track cart trips, supply runs, room entries and exits
- Note anomalies (wrong supplies, missing supplies, process deviations)
- Export data for analysis

This is not a time-and-motion study in the old sense. It is structured observation that produces usable data without the adversarial dynamic of a stopwatch-in-the-face approach.

## 5. Building the Standard from Observation

### Step 1 — Establish the Gutters
Before the first observation, establish the bookends:
- What is the minimum board they would give a room attendant?
- What is the maximum board they would give a room attendant?
- What does an average full-shift board actually look like?

Get specific numbers. Push back on vague answers. If they say "about 14 rooms," ask: when was the last time someone did exactly 14? What did that day look like?

### Step 2 — Observe Against the Gutters
Watch a range of conditions:
- A light day (below normal volume)
- A normal day (typical board)
- A heavy day (close to maximum board)

The observation tells you whether the gutters are real. If they say max board is 18 rooms but the data shows nobody has done 18 rooms in the last six months, the gutter is wrong.

### Step 3 — Apply the Percentile Analysis
Take the historical data (RexData or equivalent) and run the percentile analysis:
- What is the distribution of actual boards completed?
- What percentage of days hit the stated maximum?
- Where is the real center of the distribution?

This produces a defensible standard that the client cannot argue with — it is built from their own data.

### Step 4 — Validate with the Room Framework
Apply the room type framework (Document 1, Section 14):
- What type of room is this property?
- What are the parameters that drive cleaning time?
- Does the observed time match what the framework would predict?
- If not, why not? (Supplies? Process? Training? Physical layout?)

### Step 5 — Build the Standard
The standard is the output:
- Credits per checkout by room type
- Credits per stayover by room type
- Fixed task allowances (linen room, cart prep, end-of-shift)
- Adjustment factors for special conditions (suites, connecting rooms, VIP prep)

## 6. The Anonymity Shell

### What It Is
Pete builds individual conversations with property staff — room attendants, supervisors, executive housekeepers — that are separate from management and from each other. People say things in those conversations that they would not say in a group or in front of their manager.

> "I build an anonymity. And build a shell around individual conversations and then people just let it out."
> — Pete Castellano

### What Comes Out
- The real reason productivity is off (not what management thinks)
- Supply and logistics problems that have never been reported
- Process shortcuts that are creating quality problems
- Staffing decisions that don't make sense to the people doing the work
- Things that were never like this before — something changed and nobody fixed it

### How to Use It
This information is used to inform the standard and the remediation plan — not to get anyone in trouble. Pete's job is to fix the operation, not audit the staff.

The framing: "You wish someone would come see how things really are. Here I am."

## 7. Findings and Recommendations

### Format
Pete thinks out loud better than he writes. AI (Claude) is used to take the raw conversation, observations, and data and produce:
- A structured findings document
- Recommendations organized by impact and feasibility
- Supporting data from the percentile analysis and observation notes
- A PowerPoint presentation if needed for executive presentation

### The Key Principle
Findings should be actionable. Not "your standards are outdated" — but "here is what your standard should be, here is why, and here is how we get there."

### Delivering Bad News
Some findings are uncomfortable:
- The standard has been wrong for years
- The previous implementation was incomplete
- Management decisions created the problem
- The software is being blamed for an operational problem

Pete delivers these directly, with data, and with a path forward. The goal is not to assign blame but to build the case for change.

## 9. Billing and Scope Management

### Scope Creep
Hospitality clients are very good at adding scope without acknowledging it. Common patterns:
- "While you're here, can you look at the restaurant too?"
- "We added a new outlet — can you just configure it?"
- "Corporate wants a report — can you pull that together?"

Pete's approach: do it once as a courtesy, document it, and fold it into the next scope conversation. Anything that becomes recurring is billable.

### Change Orders
Meal period additions, new properties, new modules — these are change orders, not scope creep if handled correctly. The Rosewood/Baha Mar afternoon meal period addition is an example: Pete went back and charged for it.

## 10. Knowing When to Push Back

### When the Client Is Wrong About the Problem
If a client insists the problem is X and Pete's analysis shows it is Y, Pete says so. The client hired Pete for judgment, not agreement.

### When the Conditions Are Not Right for Training
If interfaces are not live, if staff is not available, if it is peak season — Pete pushes back on the training date. The 3-week cancellation clause exists for a reason. The cost of training under wrong conditions is always higher than the cost of rescheduling.

### When the Champion Is Not There
If the onsite champion has left, been reassigned, or checked out, Pete flags this immediately. A labor management implementation without a champion becomes shelf-ware. Pete will not deliver training into a void.

## 11. Strategic Logics — The Engagement Opening Framework

### What It Is
Strategic Logics is a facilitated executive discussion process developed by Mac McIntire (Innovative Management Group, Las Vegas). It walks a leadership team through nine elements of their business in a specific logical order — Product, Customer, Competitor, Marketing, Economic, Quality/Service, Resources/Technology, Organizational, Human Resource — building unified strategic understanding and surfacing philosophical disagreements that block alignment.

Mac gifted Pete his entire body of work, including Strategic Logics, before his passing in November 2023. Pete holds the right to use, adapt, and deploy all of it. Derivatives are © 2026 Pete Castellano / Platinum Workforce Strategies, LLC.

### Why It Belongs in the Playbook
Most labor management engagements fail not because the software is wrong or the standard is wrong — but because the organization is not aligned around what it is trying to accomplish. Managers don't trust the standard because they don't understand the why. Executives override the system because they never bought into the direction it was built to support.

Strategic Logics addresses the layer above the labor management system. It is the *why* that makes the *how* work.

### When to Use It
- **Executive team engagement** — when the client needs alignment before implementation can succeed
- **Recovery engagements** — when the system exists but isn't being used, often because nobody agreed on what they were trying to achieve
- **New client scoping** — as a diagnostic: does this leadership team know what business they're in? If not, that's the starting point
- **Larger consulting engagements** — where Pete is functioning as a strategic advisor, not just a labor management consultant

### The Connection to PWS Methodology
Mac had the religion. Pete has the science of business. Together:
- Strategic Logics provides the philosophical and strategic foundation
- PWS provides the analytical infrastructure, labor standards, and operational accountability

A client who has been through a Strategic Logics discussion understands *why* the standard is what it is. They defend it upward. They use the system proactively. They step forward together.
