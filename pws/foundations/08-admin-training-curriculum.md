## 0. Opening Framing — Why This Matters (from Ralph's 7/31/26 kickoff call)

Open every admin training with this, not the software. Sets the stakes before the mechanics.

- **The treadmill analogy:** buying/installing Unifocus doesn't help by itself — someone has to actually interact with it, or it becomes "a laundry rack" (unused, gathering clutter). The system only works if it's worked.
- **The seriousness analogy:** you wouldn't let an untrained front-desk hire "wing it" on their first split-folio guest in Opera, or a new server work without POS training. Unifocus deserves the same seriousness — it's not a side tool, it's the system the labor operation runs on.
- **The "champion" ask:** every property needs someone designated as the go-to admin/champion — and needs to re-designate promptly if that person leaves. Losing the champion without a replacement is the direct cause of "a treadmill with laundry on it."
- **Why train admins deeply now, not later:** external consultants are temporary — there's a support window, then the property is on its own. The property's own admin knowing the answers is faster than routing every question back to Unifocus.
- **The Riverside/software-company framing (new context for opening credibility, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** Unifocus was acquired by Riverside, which is pushing the company from "consulting company with software behind it" toward "software company that also does consulting" — self-service, scalable, eventually reachable by smaller select-service properties that couldn't afford the old high-touch model. Useful to set expectations: larger/complex properties still need full consulting, but the product direction is toward more self-administration over time. Also doubles as PWS rate-justification context — explains *why* deep admin training matters more now, not less, as Unifocus itself leans more self-service.

## 1. Core Vocabulary & Mental Models (reusable in any admin session)

The conceptual backbone — teach these before any hands-on screen work, they make everything downstream click.

- **Shift** = the defined worked-hours window (a fixed-size bucket — start time, end time).
- **Schedule** = a shift once a person is actually attached to it (this is what triggers work-rule application, e.g. a lunch rule only fires once a schedule exists).
- **Standard** = the KBI-driven (or fixed) formula that decides how many hours of a given shift type get created for a given day.
- **Forecast** = volumes only. **Projected** = hours, standard-driven. **Schedule** = what was actually built/published. **Budget** = a wholly separate, manually-loaded data set.
- **The one-line summary (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript) — "It really takes two things to make a labor standard work: volumes and a plan."** Good opening line for §1 itself — everything else in this module is really just unpacking those two words. Pairs with the flip-the-model line below.
- **"We do not schedule people" — the flip that makes troubleshooting click.** The standard generates *shifts* based on volume; the system then *fills* those shifts using employee data and work rules. Managers experience it as "scheduling a person," but the actual sequence is need-first, person-second. Pete calls this out explicitly as the thing that "a lot of people still don't get" — worth stating directly rather than letting attendees infer it, since troubleshooting (including for admins) gets much easier once this clicks.

### The "Pitcher and Glasses" model — how standards fill shifts
Think of total standard hours for a day as a pitcher of water, and each defined shift length as a glass of fixed size. The system fills one glass completely before starting the next — it does **not** spread hours evenly across all available shift slots. If the underlying shift buckets are inconsistent (e.g., mixing 6-hour and 6.5-hour definitions under one shift record), the pour lands in an unintended combination.

### Rounding threshold
Defaults to 20% of the job's minimum shift length (typically ~0.8 hours on a 4-hour minimum) before the system creates one more shift rather than stopping. Configurable per job. Matters most on productivity/volume-driven standards where hours accumulate in small increments.

### Minutes-per-unit standards — the two-pass mechanic (a frequent "is this broken?" question)
A minutes-per-unit standard calculates total minutes needed in one pass, then pours that total into shift-length "cups" in a second, separate pass — it does **not** recombine a leftover partial shift back into the pool. Practical consequence: **expect at least one short shift out of the box** on any minutes-per-unit standard, and the system tends to hand out short shifts first. This is expected behavior, not a misconfiguration. **First troubleshooting step on any productivity-based standard: look for the short shifts and evaluate whether to expand them** — don't assume something's broken.
- **"Filling the pitcher with an eyedropper" (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)** — Pete's live description of why minutes-per-unit standards feel more volatile than daily-hours standards: every single occupied/checkout/stayover room adds or subtracts individually before any shift even generates, versus a flat KPI threshold. Good framing for explaining to a room why this standard type looks "twitchier" than others.
- **Staff Minutes is a genuine second pass, not a blend:** the system pours primary cleaning minutes (departures/stayovers) into shifts first, *then* separately runs a second, smaller pass adding non-cleaning time (stand-up meetings, cart trips) as its own additional pitcher poured into new or existing cups — it does not fold non-cleaning time back into the first pass and re-pour everything together.
- **Rounding threshold, restated precisely from a live walkthrough:** the ~20% ("point two") threshold is checked against the *last partial cup specifically* — below it, the leftover invisibly distributes across existing shifts (they run slightly long); at or above it, a new minimum-length shift gets created. This is the same 20% figure as the Rounding Threshold section above, just observed live on a real Room Attendant standard — corroborates the mechanic rather than adding a new one.

### The negative-hours trick
Enter a negative number directly into a standard line to net out labor already accounted for elsewhere (e.g., turndown attendants doing partial room-cleaning work already counted under Room Attendant). Not visibly expressed anywhere else in the system — pure internal netting. Alternative: have the employee clock into the correct secondary job code instead. Either works; **the important thing is picking one method per property and staying consistent.**

### Actual vs. Standard is the meaningful comparison — not vs. Schedule or Projected
Projected-vs-schedule is secondary/interesting only. Actual-vs-standard is what validates whether the operation is running to plan. Corollary: **standards work forwards and backwards** — forward, they generate projected hours/shifts for planning; backward, actual-vs-standard is how you decide whether a manager's recurring manual exception should be formalized into the standard itself ("if a manager keeps adding the same kind of exception, that should be your plan").

### Flow Patterns and Float standards — distributing volume within a shift (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)
Distinct from the Pitcher-and-Glasses model above (that's about pouring total hours into shift-length buckets) — this is about **when within a shift** volume actually happens. A flow pattern tells the system how a day's total volume (e.g., 100 covers) distributes across 15-minute intervals, so a **float standard** can staff up to the interval peak and taper down on the back side, instead of a flat shift-long headcount. Not every property has these configured — worth checking for before assuming a job only needs a fixed-length shift.

### Environments — forecasting-only, not a general-purpose tool
Environments exist solely to help forecast volumes (tagging analogous past/future dates for irregular events) — they don't do anything else in the system. Requires real diligence (tagging both the past analogous date and the future date), which makes them advanced/manual-intensive — not recommended for routine use, reserve for genuinely irregular volume events.

### Banquet standards are inherently an estimate, not a precise calculation
The system isn't reading BEOs — it only sees broad daily totals, not event-type detail or event count. Consequence: don't over-scrutinize a single banquet day's variance. The goal is landing on budget over time and being able to **explain variance** at the weekly labor meeting using knowledge of how the standard was built — not achieving daily precision. Separately: standard hours for banquet culinary/stewarding/setup all land on the event date even though the real labor is spent over the days leading up to it — evaluate those roles over the whole week, not day-by-day.
- **Units per shift — the third standard type, alongside daily-hours and minutes-per-unit (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** ratio-based (e.g., 1 server per 20 covers for breakfast/lunch, 1 per 15 for plated dinner) — matches how banquet staff actually think about staffing, which is *why* the KBI granularity work (breakfast/lunch/dinner × plated/buffet/continental/box) exists in the first place: it lets the standard mirror their own mental ratio instead of forcing a translation. Makes whole shifts as soon as any volume triggers the ratio. Remember banquet volume sums across the *entire property* for that meal type/format — the system can't tell "one 100-person event" from "three simultaneous 33-person events," it just adds covers.
- **A generalizable banquet-standards completeness audit, worth running at any property:** for every event type/format combination in the KBI structure, confirm there's a standard covering **service, culinary, stewarding, AND setup** — all four, every combination. A missing combination doesn't error, it silently produces zero labor for that slice — an invisible gap, not a loud one. Worth an explicit audit pass rather than assuming coverage.
- **KPI proliferation discipline:** when a new/unusual event type shows up (a luau, a new reception tier), don't reflexively create a new KPI for it — fold it into the closest existing category unless it's genuinely different enough to warrant its own bucket. Minimize new KPIs; map to what's close enough.

### Seasonality — two distinct structural choices (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript, long-flagged as missing per the 8/2/26 update log)
- **If an entire job only exists part of the year** (e.g., a purely seasonal role), give the **job itself** a date range — no assignment needed.
- **If it's the same job/same ADP code, but the labor standard genuinely needs to differ by season** (e.g., a stayover takes longer to service in winter because of tracked-in snow/ski gear), use an **assignment** with its own date range under that job — a single job can layer several different seasonal assignments across the year.
- **Prove the need first.** Pete's stated bias: "I don't like making assignments unless we have to." Don't reflexively give every job seasonal assignments without a real operational driver — and don't assume every job's seasonal window lines up (e.g., a property's houseperson wood-gathering season can start weeks before its room-attendant seasonality shifts).
- **Why this matters more than it looks — Labor Budgeter compatibility:** manually editing a standard by hand each season (the default instinct) works for the current year but breaks Labor Budgeter, because building next year's budget in summer won't pick up a manual edit that hasn't happened yet. The date range needs to be defined *generically* (recurs every October, not "October 2026" specifically) so running the standard across a full future year automatically picks up the seasonal segment when Budgeter reaches it.
- **A third option, and Pete's actual default when unsure: no date range at all — let volume carry the seasonality.** A revenue center's KPI can simply go to zero when something's closed (an outdoor venue, a seasonal outlet) — zero means no labor, and that's a legitimate forecasting mechanism, not just a fallback. Direct quote worth reusing live: **"If you're not sure, leave it all year long and control it with your volume."** Only works for KPIs the property actually controls per revenue center (a default KPI like Occupied Rooms can't be "shut off" this way). **When to use which of the three:** whole job genuinely doesn't exist part of the year with a fixed open/close date → job-level date range. Same job, but the standard cycles on and off multiple times across shoulder seasons → this gets complicated fast with dates, so let volume drive it via a zeroed KPI instead. Same job, standard genuinely differs by a clean seasonal split (not just "busier") → assignment-level date range. **Don't confuse ordinary demand variation with seasonality** — "we're busier in summer" is just the standard reacting to volume normally; it doesn't need any date-based mechanism at all, in either direction.
- **Restraint principle, stated directly:** "I would not go hog wild on that yet" — same caution as the "prove the need first" guidance above, worth repeating because seasonality is where admins most often over-engineer prematurely.

## 2. TOC-Mapped Teaching Modules

### KBIs (Handbook pp. 18–26) ⭐
Foundation for everything downstream. Teach the independent/dependent framing: base KBIs (e.g., Market Groups/Segments = Rooms KBIs) are the independent variables; generated KBIs (e.g., Revenue Centers) are dependent, derived from those and then editable. Calculated KBIs referencing an empty input KBI silently produce zero output — a frequent root cause of "why is this showing zero" tickets (see Rosewood Baha Mar case study below).
- **Regression vs. TES — the mechanics worth teaching explicitly (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** both look back only 13 weeks — no more, no less. **TES** (trend-adjusted exponential smoothing) is a weighted average — it will *always* produce a number, but can't sense a cliff: coming out of a busy stretch, it keeps predicting busy even as volume craters, because it's an average, not a relationship. **Regression** works off a capture ratio (e.g., covers as a ratio of guests) — more responsive to real relationship shifts, but more failure-prone: if the underlying relationship breaks down, regression throws a zero *on purpose* rather than guessing. Practical teaching line: "TES will always give you something — I trust it less. Regression could give you a better number, but it'll throw a zero if something gets out of whack." Whoever edits the revenue center forecast needs to know which method is driving it before trusting the number blindly.
- **Environments, concretely:** used to tag and skip anomalous history from a TES/regression calculation (e.g., a blizzard week) so it doesn't pollute the 13-week average — and to later forecast a *future* anomalous date by pointing it at prior analogous ones. Isolate-and-skip is the core mechanic. Also configurable to ignore day-of-week matching (normally a holiday only looks back at the last 13 instances of the *same weekday*) — real setup effort, "running, not walking"; don't over-engineer it since the result is still just a number someone edits anyway.
- **The "fake file" mapping trick (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** the KBI mapping screen can only show source values it has actually seen arrive in a real file — you can't pre-map something the system has never received. Workaround: build one throwaway file containing every possible value the property might ever send (e.g., every banquet meal-type/format combination), import it once to light up the full pulldown, map everything in one pass, then delete the file. Far faster than mapping piecemeal as each new value happens to show up over weeks.

### Labor Structure (Handbook pp. 39–48) ⭐
- **Two analogies that land well live (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** "It's like a Christmas tree — everything hangs off the labor structure" (employees, user administration/security, standards all attach here, so build it first). Also: "It's your chart of accounts for labor" — useful phrasing specifically for finance-minded attendees (DOFs, controllers) who already think in chart-of-accounts terms.
- **"We're kind of a slave to ADP"** — whatever ADP calls a job, Unifocus treats as a job; organization can differ slightly, but the job-level correspondence has to stay 1:1. Unifocus does have tools to merge/consolidate scattered ADP jobs (e.g., cooks split across three ADP kitchen codes) without dragging in unrelated jobs.
- Structure nests like folders (property → division → department → job), but **the real functionality lives at the job level** — everything else (KBIs, standards, hours, reporting) attaches there.
- **Moving things is risky** — dragging a job/department/division can break things or drag unintended dependencies along.
- **Job naming discipline matters beyond cosmetics:** name every outlet-specific job with a short property/outlet identifier (e.g., "BQT Server" not just "Server") — floating employees and any external reporting depend on job *names* being unique and stable, since there's often no separate job-code join key.
- **Jobs and KBIs cannot be deleted once used, full stop — and it's not worth fighting (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript, vivid phrasing worth reusing live):** "it's like a tattoo — once it's been used anywhere, it's stuck." Getting Unifocus support to actually remove a used job takes real time even by ticket ("an act of Congress," ~a month turnaround) because history and dependencies have to be untangled first. **Pete's stated recommendation: don't even file the ticket.** Leave the job in place (preserves year-over-year reporting integrity — moving it to a Do-Not-Use bucket breaks historical comparisons), just make it invisible going forward: uncheck "show on dashboard," and/or remove it from specific users' visible labor structure under User Administration. Reinforces the existing "get naming right before first use" guidance below with a concrete why.
- **Jobs vs. Assignments — a distinction worth teaching as its own topic, frequently confused (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** the job itself must be 1:1 with ADP (see "slave to ADP" above) — but an **assignment** under a job lets you vary labor treatment for a sub-condition of that same ADP job without creating a second job. Two legitimate uses: (1) **volume/day-sensitive labor** — e.g., an Omelet Station assignment under Cook that only generates standard hours on Sundays over 50 covers, while the base Cook standard stays flat; (2) **a pure display label with no separate standard** — purely cosmetic (e.g., showing "Pizza/Pasta Station" on the printed schedule) purely to help a manager adopt the system by showing what they're used to seeing, with zero labor-standard logic attached. **The critical limitation to teach explicitly: there is no reporting or labor-spend visibility below the job level** — everything at the assignment level rolls up into the job with no drill-down. **Common misconfiguration to watch for:** properties sometimes try to map actual ADP job numbers onto assignments (flipping the model upside down, organizing around how a manager thinks about the work rather than how payroll/accounting does) — this breaks the reporting model and should be corrected back to one job per ADP job, assignments only for sub-condition labor logic or display labels.
- **A concrete test for "should this be its own department?" (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** when debating whether an outlet (e.g., a bar/lounge) should be pulled out into its own labor-structure department rather than nested under a larger outlet, check whether it's already a separate department in ADP / on the P&L. If yes, that's the tie-breaker — the labor structure should match. Reporting can still technically work either way, but it's "not as clean" when the structures diverge.

### Labor Standards (Handbook pp. 49–58) ⭐ — most complex section
- **Settings cascade down the hierarchy:** set boilerplate once at division level (min/max shift, KBI-related flag); only exceptions need managing below that.
- **Shift naming convention:** `[Shift Name] [Start]-[End, 4-digit military time] (worked hours in parentheses)`. Once inside the Labor Standards grid, the shift's actual start/end time isn't visible — the parenthetical is the only way to sanity-check hours without flipping screens. Follow this every time; it's what catches the exact error class below.
- **The shift record must match what its own name claims — apply this doc's Module 3 (Modifying Existing Standards) whenever it doesn't.**
- **Work Rules change effective shift length**, not the shift itself (8-hour shift + 30-min unpaid lunch = 8.5-hour *schedule*, standard is still 8 hours). Watch for "at X hours" vs. "over X hours" break-trigger settings — easy to get backwards with real consequences.
- **"Fifty shades of shifts" — a key design choice attendees rarely realize they're making (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** a standard can be built to *schedule* (many discrete shifts matching how you actually staff, e.g. ten people across fifty shifts) or to *run-rate* (the same total hours expressed as one continuous coverage block, e.g. "168 hours a week on this job"). Neither is wrong — but pick deliberately based on whether the standard needs to drive real scheduling or just needs to validate budgeted hours, and know which one you're looking at when reading a standard.
- **"The wall" — don't reforecast backward.** Once a planning week has generated shifts and those shifts have been filled/scheduled, treat that boundary as a wall: administrators technically *can* go back and edit the forecast/regenerate, but doing so risks silently clearing an already-published, manager-edited schedule. If a schedule "looks wrong," troubleshoot forward in Employee Maintenance, don't reach back over the wall by default.

### Labor Budgeter (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)
Distinct from day-to-day forecasting: feed it day-by-day volume for an entire *future* year, and it applies existing labor standards to calculate hours and dollars by month by job — an automated first-pass labor budget. **Prerequisite, stated as a hard gate: "you have to trust your standards first."** Realistic adoption timeline is about **two years** of practicing alongside a property's existing budget process (year one: run it as a second set of eyes only; year two-plus: consider actually building the budget from it) — set this expectation up front so it doesn't read as a quick win.

### Planning (Handbook pp. 60–76) ⭐
Weekly workflow: forecast → generate revenue centers → generate projected hours → generate/publish schedule. Teach it as a forward pipeline, then immediately pair it with the backward validation loop (actual vs. standard) from Module 1 — admins should think of planning as a loop, not a one-way process.

### Work Rules (Handbook pp. 78–83)
State law is the floor, not the answer — a property's own policy can be stricter than the state minimum, and "no state requirement" doesn't mean "no rule needed." Name every work rule to describe what it does in the title itself; the system doesn't surface this clearly elsewhere.

### Employee Maintenance (Handbook pp. 85–99) ⭐
Lower priority for a *modify-existing-standards* session specifically — cover only if data-quality issues are actively blocking a standard from calculating correctly (e.g., missing job effective dates).
- **"Field promotion" (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)** — Pete's name for departments scheduling a brand-new hire before the ADP→Unifocus employee import has caught up ("ask forgiveness later"). A recognizable, funny-because-true framing for a very common admin support ticket — worth using live, it disarms what's otherwise a frustrating recurring complaint.
- **Why import salaried employees too, even though they're not scheduled/standard-driven:** helps with future Labor Budgeter use, and lets a labor meeting/report reveal when hourly staff are quietly covering for an absent salaried role — a useful HR-visibility side benefit, not just a budgeting one.
- **Standing recommendation, worth giving every admin day-one (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** the ADP→Unifocus import runs employees first, then hours, as two separate daily processes — either can silently fail per-employee if a job doesn't exist yet. Unifocus can send daily error emails for both ("Employee Reconcile" and "Hours Transfer") but only to whoever's subscribed — many admins don't know these exist or aren't on them. **Simple, fast support-ticket ask:** "please add [names] to the Employee Reconcile and Hours Transfer notification emails." Cheap way to catch import failures same-day instead of discovering them downstream as a missing-standard-hours mystery.
- **Two distinct error tiers on the hours-import side worth distinguishing when troubleshooting (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** (1) the job doesn't exist in Unifocus at all — the employee doesn't import, no labor data flows anywhere; (2) the employee and job both exist, but nothing authorizes that employee for that specific job — this one still drops hours correctly into labor reporting (so reports stay accurate) even though the error persists on the reconcile report until fixed. Knowing which tier you're looking at tells you whether reporting is actually compromised or just the error queue is noisy.
- **The "lie to it" backdating trick, for clearing an error retroactively:** hours imports carry a rolling ~21-day window, so fixing a job/authorization gap in ADP today only stops *new* errors — it doesn't retroactively clear errors already queued for the prior weeks in that window. Backdating the effective date on the corrected secondary job assignment in Unifocus (setting it earlier than today, to match when the work actually started) clears the historical error queue immediately instead of waiting for it to age out.
- **Same-day job-creation SOP, reinforced from a live example:** a property created "Fitness Desk Supervisor" in ADP and assigned an employee to it same-day, but the mirrored job was never created in Unifocus — surfaced later as a "home job must be valid" profile error. Reinforces the standing "slave to ADP" rule (§2 Labor Structure): whoever creates a job in ADP must trigger same-day creation in Unifocus, ideally via an explicit SOP naming who does the Unifocus half if it isn't the same person (many properties' HR teams don't know the Unifocus side needs to happen at all).

## 3. Modifying Existing Standards — The Audit Method

**This is the module to lead with whenever the engagement is "fix what's here," not "build from scratch"** — distinct from a green-field implementation and worth naming explicitly as its own skill.

1. **Read the shift record before touching the standard.** Does its defined length match what its own name/parenthetical claims? (Real example: a shift named for a 6.5-hour bucket but carrying 12 hours of standard — the system poured it as one 6.5-hour shift + one 5.5-hour leftover, not the two 6.5-hour shifts the name implied.)
2. **On any productivity/minutes-per-unit standard, look for the short shift first.** It's the expected artifact of the two-pass mechanic (Module 1), not proof of a bug — start troubleshooting there before assuming misconfiguration.
3. **Check the rounding threshold** is behaving as expected given the shift's minimum length.
4. **Validate against Actual vs. Standard trend over multiple weeks**, not a single day — a standard being off on one day doesn't mean it's broken; sustained variance does.
5. **If a manager is manually adding the same exception shift repeatedly, that's the signal to formalize it into the standard** rather than leaving it as a recurring manual override.
6. **Standard changes don't retroactively fix anything already generated** — editing a standard doesn't touch already-generated projected hours/schedules. The system regenerates automatically ~4x/day; a manual "Regenerate Projected Hours" tests changes immediately but risks clearing the existing schedule for that job — use cautiously, especially on a live property.
