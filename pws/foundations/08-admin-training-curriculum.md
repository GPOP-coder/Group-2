## 0. Opening Framing — Why This Matters (from Ralph's 7/31/26 kickoff call)

Open every admin training with this, not the software. Sets the stakes before the mechanics.

- **The treadmill analogy:** buying/installing Unifocus doesn't help by itself — someone has to actually interact with it, or it becomes "a laundry rack" (unused, gathering clutter). The system only works if it's worked.
- **The seriousness analogy:** you wouldn't let an untrained front-desk hire "wing it" on their first split-folio guest in Opera, or a new server work without POS training. Unifocus deserves the same seriousness — it's not a side tool, it's the system the labor operation runs on.
- **The "champion" ask:** every property needs someone designated as the go-to admin/champion — and needs to re-designate promptly if that person leaves. Losing the champion without a replacement is the direct cause of "a treadmill with laundry on it."
- **Why train admins deeply now, not later:** external consultants are temporary — there's a support window, then the property is on its own. The property's own admin knowing the answers is faster than routing every question back to Unifocus.
- **The Riverside/software-company framing (new context for opening credibility, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** Unifocus was acquired by Riverside, which is pushing the company from "consulting company with software behind it" toward "software company that also does consulting" — self-service, scalable, eventually reachable by smaller select-service properties that couldn't afford the old high-touch model. Useful to set expectations: larger/complex properties still need full consulting, but the product direction is toward more self-administration over time. Also doubles as PWS rate-justification context — explains *why* deep admin training matters more now, not less, as Unifocus itself leans more self-service.

## 1. Core Vocabulary & Mental Models (reusable in any admin session)

The conceptual backbone — teach these before any hands-on screen work, they make everything downstream click.

- **Shift** = the defined worked-hours window (a fixed-size bucket — start time, end time).
- **Schedule** = a shift once a person is actually attached to it (this is what triggers work-rule application, e.g. a lunch rule only fires once a schedule exists).
- **Standard** = the KBI-driven (or fixed) formula that decides how many hours of a given shift type get created for a given day.
- **Forecast** = volumes only. **Projected** = hours, standard-driven. **Schedule** = what was actually built/published. **Budget** = a wholly separate, manually-loaded data set.
- **The one-line summary (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript) — "It really takes two things to make a labor standard work: volumes and a plan."** Good opening line for §1 itself — everything else in this module is really just unpacking those two words. Pairs with the flip-the-model line below.
- **The fuller philosophy behind that line, worth stating explicitly at the very start of any admin session (source: 08-03 PM Meeting, Pete's own words): "This whole thing is pegged on volumes. Your customers are running your business — not how many employees I have, who's off, who's on. We start with the customers, and the volume of customers runs the business."** Comply with that framing and staffing follows naturally; fight it (staffing to headcount availability instead of demand) and you get both unhappy guests and unhappy overstaffed employees fighting over too few covers.
- **"We do not schedule people" — the flip that makes troubleshooting click.** The standard generates *shifts* based on volume; the system then *fills* those shifts using employee data and work rules. Managers experience it as "scheduling a person," but the actual sequence is need-first, person-second. Pete calls this out explicitly as the thing that "a lot of people still don't get" — worth stating directly rather than letting attendees infer it, since troubleshooting (including for admins) gets much easier once this clicks.

### The "Pitcher and Glasses" model — how standards fill shifts
Think of total standard hours for a day as a pitcher of water, and each defined shift length as a glass of fixed size. The system fills one glass completely before starting the next — it does **not** spread hours evenly across all available shift slots. If the underlying shift buckets are inconsistent (e.g., mixing 6-hour and 6.5-hour definitions under one shift record), the pour lands in an unintended combination.

### Rounding threshold
Defaults to 20% of the job's minimum shift length (typically ~0.8 hours on a 4-hour minimum) before the system creates one more shift rather than stopping. Configurable per job. Matters most on productivity/volume-driven standards where hours accumulate in small increments.

### Minutes-per-unit standards — the two-pass mechanic (a frequent "is this broken?" question)
A minutes-per-unit standard calculates total minutes needed in one pass, then pours that total into shift-length "cups" in a second, separate pass — it does **not** recombine a leftover partial shift back into the pool. Practical consequence: **expect at least one short shift out of the box** on any minutes-per-unit standard, and the system tends to hand out short shifts first. This is expected behavior, not a misconfiguration. **First troubleshooting step on any productivity-based standard: look for the short shifts and evaluate whether to expand them** — don't assume something's broken.
- **"Filling the pitcher with an eyedropper" (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)** — Pete's live description of why minutes-per-unit standards feel more volatile than daily-hours standards: every single occupied/checkout/stayover room adds or subtracts individually before any shift even generates, versus a flat KBI threshold. Good framing for explaining to a room why this standard type looks "twitchier" than others.
- **Staff Minutes is a genuine second pass, not a blend:** the system pours primary cleaning minutes (departures/stayovers) into shifts first, *then* separately runs a second, smaller pass adding non-cleaning time (stand-up meetings, cart trips) as its own additional pitcher poured into new or existing cups — it does not fold non-cleaning time back into the first pass and re-pour everything together.
- **Rounding threshold, restated precisely from a live walkthrough:** the ~20% ("point two") threshold is checked against the *last partial cup specifically* — below it, the leftover invisibly distributes across existing shifts (they run slightly long); at or above it, a new minimum-length shift gets created. This is the same 20% figure as the Rounding Threshold section above, just observed live on a real Room Attendant standard — corroborates the mechanic rather than adding a new one.

### The negative-hours trick
Enter a negative number directly into a standard line to net out labor already accounted for elsewhere (e.g., turndown attendants doing partial room-cleaning work already counted under Room Attendant). Not visibly expressed anywhere else in the system — pure internal netting. Alternative: have the employee clock into the correct secondary job code instead. Either works; **the important thing is picking one method per property and staying consistent.**

### Actual vs. Standard is the meaningful comparison — not vs. Schedule or Projected
Projected-vs-schedule is secondary/interesting only. Actual-vs-standard is what validates whether the operation is running to plan. Corollary: **standards work forwards and backwards** — forward, they generate projected hours/shifts for planning; backward, actual-vs-standard is how you decide whether a manager's recurring manual exception should be formalized into the standard itself ("if a manager keeps adding the same kind of exception, that should be your plan").
- **The two questions every labor meeting should answer (source: 08-03 PM Meeting AI notes — confirmed as Pete's standing teaching structure, corroborated independently in the 08-03 AM Lecture transcript too):** *"How did we do last week?"* and *"Are we ready for next week?"* — one backward-looking, one forward-looking, and that's the whole meeting. Goal is a single, self-directed midweek meeting, not separate review/planning sessions. The person running the meeting owns data accuracy *and* the narrative behind the numbers (weather, cancellations, one-off events) — a report without that context invites the wrong conclusion. **The definitive version of this line, direct quote, worth using verbatim (source: 08-03 PM Meeting transcript, Pete explicitly flags it as his own overlay, "this isn't even on the deck"): "How did we do last week? Are we ready for next week? Period. That's it. Everything else comes underneath that."**
- **"Self-directed" labor meetings, with a real follow-up mechanism (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** "I like self-directed labor meetings, where you ask the questions and then things get resolved." When something's off and it's a repeat pattern (not a one-off), it needs a real follow-up with a due date and a feedback loop — could be as small as adjusting a start time, or flagging a vacancy driving overtime. **The split of ownership: the person running the meeting owns prompting and tracking; resolving and following through is on the department.**
- **Standards can drift when scheduling and budgeting standards are kept separate (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** it's possible to maintain one standard tuned for real scheduling and a separate one tuned for budget validation, but the two can quietly drift apart over time if not reconciled periodically — worth flagging as a risk the first time a property proposes splitting them (ties to the "fifty shades of shifts" schedule-vs-run-rate choice above — that's a single-standard design choice; this is the multi-standard version of the same tension).
- **Standard changes take 2–3 years to fully work through a property's budget cycle (source: 08-03 PM Meeting) — a different timeline than the Labor Budgeter tool-adoption timeline below.** This is about the downstream consequence of *changing an existing standard*, not about adopting the Budgeter tool for the first time. Reinforces why standard changes shouldn't be made casually — get explicit approval and understand the multi-year ripple before changing something that's already feeding a budget.
- **Explainable variance, not a new standard (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** infrequent minor issues and known special events (Mother's Day, a one-off banquet surge) should be explained at the labor meeting using narrative context, not used as justification to build a new standard around them. Reinforces the existing "banquet standards are inherently an estimate" guidance above — the fix for rare variance is usually a sentence at the meeting, not a system change. **Live example worth reusing verbatim:** a manager asks for a special standard just for Mother's Day — "It's called explainable variance." Know going in that outlier days aren't well-represented in the standard, and that's fine — the standard doesn't need to explain the day, the person running the meeting does.
- **A clean diagnostic one-liner for splitting forecasting failure from execution failure (source: 08-03 PM Meeting, direct quote): "If your standard hours are out of alignment, maybe your forecasting was bad. If your actual hours are out of alignment, maybe your execution was bad."** Gives a manager a concrete first branch point instead of just "something's off."
- **A second diagnostic split, specifically for overtime (source: 08-03 PM Meeting): if a department is running over standard AND running overtime, that's a management problem. If it's running its standard but needs overtime to get there, that's an HR problem — not enough people to meet the plan everyone already agreed to.** Worth teaching as its own check on the Labor Effectiveness Report's overtime-by-position detail — hours can look fine in aggregate while overtime specifically is what's actually driving the variance.
- **Scheduled vs. Actual is a legitimate secondary report, but don't make it the headline metric — and don't assume unusual scheduling patterns are wrong (source: 08-03 PM Meeting, Pete's own casino background):** once a department is consistently hitting its standard, it's fine to let go of *how* they got there, even when the pattern looks culturally unusual on its face. Example: casino cocktail servers scheduled to double up every weekend regardless of early-month volume, because "you schedule them, they're coming, no matter what" is that department's actual operating culture — versus cage cashiers, a precision-driven culture managing a $2M drawer to the penny. Different departments can have entirely different relationships to their own schedule; the standard, not the scheduling pattern, is the thing to hold them to.

### Flow Patterns and Float standards — distributing volume within a shift (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)
Distinct from the Pitcher-and-Glasses model above (that's about pouring total hours into shift-length buckets) — this is about **when within a shift** volume actually happens. A flow pattern tells the system how a day's total volume (e.g., 100 covers) distributes across 15-minute intervals, so a **float standard** can staff up to the interval peak and taper down on the back side, instead of a flat shift-long headcount. Not every property has these configured — worth checking for before assuming a job only needs a fixed-length shift.

### Environments — forecasting-only, not a general-purpose tool
Environments exist solely to help forecast volumes (tagging analogous past/future dates for irregular events) — they don't do anything else in the system. Requires real diligence (tagging both the past analogous date and the future date), which makes them advanced/manual-intensive — not recommended for routine use, reserve for genuinely irregular volume events.

### Banquet standards are inherently an estimate, not a precise calculation
The system isn't reading BEOs — it only sees broad daily totals, not event-type detail or event count. Consequence: don't over-scrutinize a single banquet day's variance. The goal is landing on budget over time and being able to **explain variance** at the weekly labor meeting using knowledge of how the standard was built — not achieving daily precision. Separately: standard hours for banquet culinary/stewarding/setup all land on the event date even though the real labor is spent over the days leading up to it — evaluate those roles over the whole week, not day-by-day.
- **Units per shift — the third standard type, alongside daily-hours and minutes-per-unit (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** ratio-based (e.g., 1 server per 20 covers for breakfast/lunch, 1 per 15 for plated dinner) — matches how banquet staff actually think about staffing, which is *why* the KBI granularity work (breakfast/lunch/dinner × plated/buffet/continental/box) exists in the first place: it lets the standard mirror their own mental ratio instead of forcing a translation. Makes whole shifts as soon as any volume triggers the ratio. Remember banquet volume sums across the *entire property* for that meal type/format — the system can't tell "one 100-person event" from "three simultaneous 33-person events," it just adds covers.
- **A generalizable banquet-standards completeness audit, worth running at any property:** for every event type/format combination in the KBI structure, confirm there's a standard covering **service, culinary, stewarding, AND setup** — all four, every combination. A missing combination doesn't error, it silently produces zero labor for that slice — an invisible gap, not a loud one. Worth an explicit audit pass rather than assuming coverage.
- **KBI proliferation discipline:** when a new/unusual event type shows up (a luau, a new reception tier), don't reflexively create a new KBI for it — fold it into the closest existing category unless it's genuinely different enough to warrant its own bucket. Minimize new KBIs; map to what's close enough.

### Seasonality — two distinct structural choices (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript, long-flagged as missing per the 8/2/26 update log)
- **If an entire job only exists part of the year** (e.g., a purely seasonal role), give the **job itself** a date range — no assignment needed.
- **If it's the same job/same ADP code, but the labor standard genuinely needs to differ by season** (e.g., a stayover takes longer to service in winter because of tracked-in snow/ski gear), use an **assignment** with its own date range under that job — a single job can layer several different seasonal assignments across the year.
- **Prove the need first.** Pete's stated bias: "I don't like making assignments unless we have to." Don't reflexively give every job seasonal assignments without a real operational driver — and don't assume every job's seasonal window lines up (e.g., a property's houseperson wood-gathering season can start weeks before its room-attendant seasonality shifts).
- **Why this matters more than it looks — Labor Budgeter compatibility:** manually editing a standard by hand each season (the default instinct) works for the current year but breaks Labor Budgeter, because building next year's budget in summer won't pick up a manual edit that hasn't happened yet. The date range needs to be defined *generically* (recurs every October, not "October 2026" specifically) so running the standard across a full future year automatically picks up the seasonal segment when Budgeter reaches it.
- **A third option, and Pete's actual default when unsure: no date range at all — let volume carry the seasonality.** A revenue center's KBI can simply go to zero when something's closed (an outdoor venue, a seasonal outlet) — zero means no labor, and that's a legitimate forecasting mechanism, not just a fallback. Direct quote worth reusing live: **"If you're not sure, leave it all year long and control it with your volume."** Only works for KBIs the property actually controls per revenue center (a default KBI like Occupied Rooms can't be "shut off" this way). **When to use which of the three:** whole job genuinely doesn't exist part of the year with a fixed open/close date → job-level date range. Same job, but the standard cycles on and off multiple times across shoulder seasons → this gets complicated fast with dates, so let volume drive it via a zeroed KBI instead. Same job, standard genuinely differs by a clean seasonal split (not just "busier") → assignment-level date range. **Don't confuse ordinary demand variation with seasonality** — "we're busier in summer" is just the standard reacting to volume normally; it doesn't need any date-based mechanism at all, in either direction.
- **Restraint principle, stated directly:** "I would not go hog wild on that yet" — same caution as the "prove the need first" guidance above, worth repeating because seasonality is where admins most often over-engineer prematurely.

## 2. TOC-Mapped Teaching Modules

### KBIs (Handbook pp. 18–26) ⭐
Foundation for everything downstream. Teach the independent/dependent framing: base KBIs (e.g., Market Groups/Segments = Rooms KBIs) are the independent variables; generated KBIs (e.g., Revenue Centers) are dependent, derived from those and then editable. Calculated KBIs referencing an empty input KBI silently produce zero output — a frequent root cause of "why is this showing zero" tickets (see Rosewood Baha Mar case study below).
- **Regression vs. TES — the mechanics worth teaching explicitly (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** both look back only 13 weeks — no more, no less. **TES** (trend-adjusted exponential smoothing) is a weighted average — it will *always* produce a number, but can't sense a cliff: coming out of a busy stretch, it keeps predicting busy even as volume craters, because it's an average, not a relationship. **Regression** works off a capture ratio (e.g., covers as a ratio of guests) — more responsive to real relationship shifts, but more failure-prone: if the underlying relationship breaks down, regression throws a zero *on purpose* rather than guessing. Practical teaching line: "TES will always give you something — I trust it less. Regression could give you a better number, but it'll throw a zero if something gets out of whack." Whoever edits the revenue center forecast needs to know which method is driving it before trusting the number blindly.
- **Environments, concretely:** used to tag and skip anomalous history from a TES/regression calculation (e.g., a blizzard week) so it doesn't pollute the 13-week average — and to later forecast a *future* anomalous date by pointing it at prior analogous ones. Isolate-and-skip is the core mechanic. Also configurable to ignore day-of-week matching (normally a holiday only looks back at the last 13 instances of the *same weekday*) — real setup effort, "running, not walking"; don't over-engineer it since the result is still just a number someone edits anyway.
- **The "fake file" mapping trick (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** the KBI mapping screen can only show source values it has actually seen arrive in a real file — you can't pre-map something the system has never received. Workaround: build one throwaway file containing every possible value the property might ever send (e.g., every banquet meal-type/format combination), import it once to light up the full pulldown, map everything in one pass, then delete the file. Far faster than mapping piecemeal as each new value happens to show up over weeks.

### Labor Structure (Handbook pp. 39–48) ⭐
- **Two analogies that land well live (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** "It's like a Christmas tree — everything hangs off the labor structure" (employees, user administration/security, standards all attach here, so build it first). Also: "It's your chart of accounts for labor" — useful phrasing specifically for finance-minded attendees (DOFs, controllers) who already think in chart-of-accounts terms.
- **"We're kind of a slave to ADP"** — whatever ADP calls a job, Unifocus treats as a job; organization can differ slightly, but the job-level correspondence has to stay 1:1. Unifocus does have tools to merge/consolidate scattered ADP jobs (e.g., cooks split across three ADP kitchen codes) without dragging in unrelated jobs.
- Structure nests like folders (property → division → department → job), but **the real functionality lives at the job level** — everything else (KBIs, standards, hours, reporting) attaches there.
- **Moving things is risky** — dragging a job/department/division can break things or drag unintended dependencies along.
- **Job naming discipline matters beyond cosmetics:** name every outlet-specific job with a short property/outlet identifier (e.g., "BQT Server" not just "Server") — floating employees and any external reporting depend on job *names* being unique and stable, since there's often no separate job-code join key.
- **Jobs and KBIs cannot be deleted once used, full stop — and it's not worth fighting (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript, vivid phrasing worth reusing live):** "it's like a tattoo — once it's been used anywhere, it's stuck." Getting Unifocus support to actually remove a used job takes real time even by ticket ("an act of Congress," ~a month turnaround) because history and dependencies have to be untangled first. **Pete's stated recommendation: don't even file the ticket.** Leave the job in place (preserves year-over-year reporting integrity — moving it to a Do-Not-Use bucket breaks historical comparisons), just make it invisible going forward: uncheck "show on dashboard," and/or remove it from specific users' visible labor structure under User Administration. Reinforces the existing "get naming right before first use" guidance below with a concrete why.
- **Jobs vs. Assignments — a distinction worth teaching as its own topic, frequently confused (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** the job itself must be 1:1 with ADP (see "slave to ADP" above) — but an **assignment** under a job lets you vary labor treatment for a sub-condition of that same ADP job without creating a second job. Two legitimate uses: (1) **volume/day-sensitive labor** — e.g., an Omelet Station assignment under Cook that only generates standard hours on Sundays over 50 covers, while the base Cook standard stays flat; (2) **a pure display label with no separate standard** — purely cosmetic (e.g., showing "Pizza/Pasta Station" on the printed schedule) purely to help a manager adopt the system by showing what they're used to seeing, with zero labor-standard logic attached. **The critical limitation to teach explicitly: there is no reporting or labor-spend visibility below the job level** — everything at the assignment level rolls up into the job with no drill-down. **Common misconfiguration to watch for:** properties sometimes try to map actual ADP job numbers onto assignments (flipping the model upside down, organizing around how a manager thinks about the work rather than how payroll/accounting does) — this breaks the reporting model and should be corrected back to one job per ADP job, assignments only for sub-condition labor logic or display labels.
- **A concrete test for "should this be its own department?" (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** when debating whether an outlet (e.g., a bar/lounge) should be pulled out into its own labor-structure department rather than nested under a larger outlet, check whether it's already a separate department in ADP / on the P&L. If yes, that's the tie-breaker — the labor structure should match. Reporting can still technically work either way, but it's "not as clean" when the structures diverge.
- **Opcodes also work as a diagnostic isolation tool, not just a cross-department combination tool (source: 08-04 F&B Labor Standards session):** when a department's headline variance number looks alarming (e.g., Main Dining showing +79% over standard), an opcode that isolates just the sub-slice actually in question (e.g., "F&B Main Dining FOH," excluding service bar) can reveal the real number is much smaller and the rest was blending from an unrelated area (here, ~9% once isolated). Worth reaching for an opcode *before* trusting a scary department-level headline number, not just when building a recurring cross-department report.
- **Operation codes (opcodes) — a lighter-weight alternative to restructuring (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** when you need to view/report on jobs that live in different labor-structure departments as one combined group (e.g., Security + Gatehouse, or all Culinary), an opcode groups them for reporting purposes *without* moving anything in the underlying structure. Cheaper and safer than a real structural move when the only need is a combined report — reach for this before restructuring jobs just to get a combined view. **The clearest example for explaining why this matters (source: 08-03 PM Meeting transcript):** an Executive Chef needs one combined view of culinary labor across every kitchen on property, but staff physically move between kitchens over time, and it's not realistic to re-code ADP every time someone moves — the individual jobs stay correctly coded to their actual ADP department, and an opcode is what lets the chef see "all my people" as one report regardless of which kitchen each shift is coded under.
- **P&L granularity is a judgment call, not a technical one (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** whether to add more account-level detail to the General Ledger (e.g., splitting one "hourly beverage" line into bartender vs. banquet-bartender) should pass a genuine "materiality test" — is the extra detail actually going to change a decision — rather than being added reflexively just because Unifocus *can* report at that granularity. Worth raising explicitly with a property's finance stakeholder rather than defaulting to maximum granularity.

### Labor Standards (Handbook pp. 49–58) ⭐ — most complex section
- **Settings cascade down the hierarchy:** set boilerplate once at division level (min/max shift, KBI-related flag); only exceptions need managing below that.
- **Shift naming convention:** `[Shift Name] [Start]-[End, 4-digit military time] (worked hours in parentheses)`. Once inside the Labor Standards grid, the shift's actual start/end time isn't visible — the parenthetical is the only way to sanity-check hours without flipping screens. Follow this every time; it's what catches the exact error class below.
- **The shift record must match what its own name claims — apply this doc's Module 3 (Modifying Existing Standards) whenever it doesn't.**
- **Work Rules change effective shift length**, not the shift itself (8-hour shift + 30-min unpaid lunch = 8.5-hour *schedule*, standard is still 8 hours). Watch for "at X hours" vs. "over X hours" break-trigger settings — easy to get backwards with real consequences.
- **"Fifty shades of shifts" — a key design choice attendees rarely realize they're making (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** a standard can be built to *schedule* (many discrete shifts matching how you actually staff, e.g. ten people across fifty shifts) or to *run-rate* (the same total hours expressed as one continuous coverage block, e.g. "168 hours a week on this job"). Neither is wrong — but pick deliberately based on whether the standard needs to drive real scheduling or just needs to validate budgeted hours, and know which one you're looking at when reading a standard.
- **"The wall" — don't reforecast backward.** Once a planning week has generated shifts and those shifts have been filled/scheduled, treat that boundary as a wall: administrators technically *can* go back and edit the forecast/regenerate, but doing so risks silently clearing an already-published, manager-edited schedule. If a schedule "looks wrong," troubleshoot forward in Employee Maintenance, don't reach back over the wall by default.
- **Overscheduling-and-cutting is a legitimate, separate practice from the standard itself — teach it as its own pattern (source: 08-04 F&B Labor Standards session):** a manager can deliberately schedule above the standard (e.g., schedule 3,600 hours against a ~2,800–3,000 standard) specifically to absorb expected call-outs, then cut down to actual need at roll call shortly before the shift (structured on-call + roll-call process). **The standard should still represent true need — the overscheduling buffer belongs outside the standard, as an intentional, managed practice, not baked into the standard's own numbers.** Worth naming as a legitimate technique when a property already does this informally, rather than treating the standard-vs-scheduled gap as a red flag by default.
- **A cost-neutral role-mix strategy worth recognizing when a property proposes it (source: 08-04 F&B Labor Standards session):** shifting hours from a higher-cost role to a lower-cost role that can absorb the same work (e.g., servers at $27–30/hr toward server assistants/bussers/runners at $18–20/hr) can hold total department labor cost flat or reduce it, without necessarily changing total scheduled hours. This is a standards-*design* decision (tighten one job's standard, loosen another's) as much as an operations one — worth recognizing as its own category distinct from a pure volume/forecast fix.
- **A concrete method for redesigning a stale ratio-based standard: work backward from real practice, not forward from a formula (source: 08-04 F&B Labor Standards session).** Rather than debating a fixed add-on rule in the abstract, find the real observed ceiling a manager actually runs to (e.g., "tops out at 17 servers around 400–425 covers") and re-derive the ratio/floor from that number — grounds the redesign in what's already proven to work operationally rather than an arbitrary adjustment.

### Labor Budgeter (new module, source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)
Distinct from day-to-day forecasting: feed it day-by-day volume for an entire *future* year, and it applies existing labor standards to calculate hours and dollars by month by job — an automated first-pass labor budget. **Prerequisite, stated as a hard gate: "you have to trust your standards first."** Realistic adoption timeline is about **two years** of practicing alongside a property's existing budget process (year one: run it as a second set of eyes only; year two-plus: consider actually building the budget from it) — set this expectation up front so it doesn't read as a quick win.
- **A concrete migration path off legacy staffing guides (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** properties that still budget off decades-old static staffing guides (Mohonk's were 30–35 years old) don't have to jump straight to full Labor Budgeter adoption. Intermediate step: shape *monthly* volume totals (covers, occupancy) into *daily* numbers using last year's actual daily patterns as the shaping curve — a more accurate starting point than a flat monthly average, and a natural bridge toward eventually retiring the legacy guide entirely once trust in the standards is there. Requires granular KBIs (e.g., breakfast split into continental/plated/buffet) to shape well, and real judgment for sporadic events like banquets that don't follow a smooth curve.
- **The core mechanic, worked live and worth demonstrating rather than just describing (source: 08-03 PM Meeting transcript):** Budgeter runs each day *individually* through the real labor standard — it does not average. Feed it a flat monthly number and a variable standard (Housekeeping especially) will "flatline" into flat daily labor, which is visibly wrong the moment you look at it. **The fix, stated in Pete's own words: "the volume changed, but the shape stayed the same"** — take the monthly total and shape it to match last year's actual daily pattern (occupancy curve, weekday/weekend mix, holiday timing), typically via a simple Excel SUM formula against last year's daily actuals, then import. All the shaping work happens outside Unifocus, in Excel; once built, reruns inside the system are cheap and fast — the setup is the real cost, not the ongoing maintenance.
- **Granularity requirement, and the shortcut that makes it tractable:** budget volumes have to come down to the *same* KBI granularity the labor standard itself actually uses (e.g., a standard differentiating breakfast continental/plated/buffet needs volume at that level, because culinary/stewarding/service each carry different effort by format) — a single monthly "Total Breakfast" number alone won't trigger the standard correctly. **Shortcut: budget one monthly total, then split it by format *percentage* (e.g., what fraction is continental vs. plated vs. buffet)** rather than forecasting each format as a fully independent number — reaches the granularity the standard needs without a separate full forecast per format.
- **Banquets need a second, distinct shaping step, and it's explicitly more art than science (source: 08-03 PM Meeting, direct quote): "There's a science to it, and then there's an art."** Real banquet activity isn't smoothly distributed across every day — it's mostly zeros with occasional spikes (maybe once or twice a week). Populating banquet covers as a flat daily number triggers labor every single day, which is wrong. The fix is shaping the daily pattern like a field of zeros with spikes matching the property's own actual rhythm — this is a genuine judgment call, not a formula, and stays that way even once every other part of the budgeting process is fully dialed in.

### Reports & Labor Meetings (new module, source: 08-03 PM Meeting — the curriculum had no dedicated reports catalog before this)
A property's report catalog maps cleanly to the two-questions framing above (§1). Teach these as a set, not one at a time, so admins know which report answers which question:
- **Actual vs. Standard (Labor Effectiveness Report)** — the core "how did we do" metric, supplementing the P&L by showing how labor cost scaled with actual volume, summarized by division/department/job over any date range. If it's on target, no further drill-down is usually needed.
- **Weekly Labor Summary** — the report managers actually value most day-to-day: day-by-day, job-by-job performance next to the KBIs driving the labor, useful for variance analysis and for quantifying the cost of one-off activities like training. For high-volume/high-variance departments (Housekeeping), teach reviewing the *weekly total* variance rather than chasing daily noise. Doubles as a two-month trend view. **Pete's own stated favorite — "this is the only report" for a manager to keep in front of them daily.** Can also be grouped by opcode (the only report that totals across multiple selected opcodes — every other report only totals one at a time). **A concrete reading order worth teaching explicitly (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** go to the bottom first and check the week's total variance, then drill into day-by-day, then job-by-job — top-down, not left-to-right.
- **Rooms Forecast Report** — the primary forecast-accuracy check: manager's forecast vs. actuals for rooms, arrivals, guests, departures. A property can look accurate on a weekly roll-up while still having real daily swings that cause scheduling pain (especially Housekeeping) — **if the forecast is trusted, a variance downstream points to a scheduling problem, not a forecasting one.** This distinction is worth stating explicitly; it changes where a manager should look when something's off. **The causal chain worth stating in exactly these words (source: 08-03 PM Meeting): "Room attendants don't clean rooms — they clean arrivals and departures."** Get arrivals wrong and departures go wrong right behind it, and Housekeeping's staffing miss follows directly — this is *why* the Rooms Forecast Report matters even to a department that never touches a room count directly. **Reading tip:** the report's market-group/segment breakdown repeats in a predictable pattern — "blur your eyes, just look at the format," and focus on the summary totals rather than parsing every repeated block.
- **Revenue Center Forecast Report** — a denser, per-revenue-center report (regression/TES stat forecast vs. any manual edit vs. actual); not necessarily run every week since the Weekly Labor Summary carries similar information, but useful for knowing whether/when a manager actually edited the system's forecast. **Whoever owns a given forecast should know which underlying method — regression or TES — is producing their number** ("should have a cheat sheet or just know that"). Grand totals on this report are literal arithmetic sums of whatever KBIs are listed (covers + revenue, etc. added together) — not always meaningful, read with that caveat. **This report should mostly answer questions, not raise them** — pre-work for whoever's editing a forecast, not something to build the meeting around.
- **Labor Productivity Report** — actual productivity (fraction-of-an-hour vs. standard); pitched at finance-level monthly/quarterly analysis and budget prep, not day-to-day operational management — don't hand this to a department manager expecting a Weekly Labor Summary substitute. **Caution worth stating explicitly (source: 08-03 PM Meeting): don't read this at single-day granularity — "an awfully fine-tooth comb."** The standard already places hours on the day of an event/checkout even though real labor spreads across surrounding days, so a single day's productivity number can mislead even when the week is fine. Best use: compare actual productivity to *budgeted* productivity monthly/quarterly, as budget-prep pre-work, without needing full Labor Budgeter setup.
- **Weekly Projected Schedule Report** — forward-looking: verifies projected hours actually align with what the standard should be producing, before the schedule gets built. **Same reading discipline as every other report here: names and dates don't matter at this level — only whether the trusted standard's required hours are actually on the schedule.** Whether the right *people* or the right *pairings* are scheduled is a separate, name-by-name management question, not something this report measures or should be expected to.
- **Labor Dashboard** — a simplified graphical rollup that can highlight cross-area variance (e.g., Rooms vs. F&B) at a glance; genuinely useful when working, but confirm it's actually functioning before leaning on it in a training session — it can silently be broken.
- **Standard weekly report packet, as a repeatable habit:** Labor Effectiveness + Weekly Labor Summary + a forecast-accuracy report (Rooms Forecast Report), pulled with key KBIs, for *every* labor meeting — not assembled ad hoc each week.
- **A recurring operational precondition, easy to overlook:** none of these reports are trustworthy unless "Generate Projected" and "Generate Standard Hours" have actually run against complete prior-week data before the meeting — late or incomplete source data is a frequent, boring root cause of "the report looks wrong," worth checking before assuming a calculation error.
- **Two report techniques worth showing live, not just describing (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** (1) on the Weekly Labor Summary, one click switches the view from a 7-day window to weekly-hours, turning the same report into a free 2-month trend view with no extra setup. (2) There's no single built-in year-over-year comparison report — **run the same report twice, once per matching date range, and compare manually.** Both are cheap, high-value habits worth demonstrating rather than just mentioning.
- **How to turn a vague variance excuse into a real conversation — "the EcoLab Training example" (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** the value of drilling into a report isn't the number itself, it's being able to quantify the story behind it. **Concrete worked example: a training session on new EcoLab cleaning products — 2 hours/person × 100 people = 200 documented hours.** If the department is actually off by 500 hours that week, "we had training" is not, on its own, a sufficient explanation — a repeated vague excuse across multiple weeks is itself a signal worth pressing on. Also doubles as a real, quantifiable input for next year's budget — an "X factor" (a recurring board meeting, a seasonal staffing ramp) that can now be planned for with an actual number instead of guessed at.
- **How to coach managers to actually use variance reports (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** frame variance reporting as constructive, not punitive — a manager who only hears about the report when something's wrong will avoid engaging with it. A balanced approach that also surfaces *positive* variance (where they beat standard) builds more genuine ownership than a purely corrective one. Consistent weekly labor meetings, done this way, are what actually build bench strength and leadership over time — worth stating explicitly as the point of the whole cadence, not just a compliance exercise. **Pete's own stated reading habit, worth demonstrating live: always start at the grand total and work backward into detail — never start at the job level and build up.** Also: deliberately don't lead with only negative variance — a manager who only ever hears what's wrong will tune the whole exercise out ("a negative Nelly").
- **Never auto-distribute a report carrying salary or dollar visibility (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** any report/schedule task can be saved as a named view and automated to email out on a cadence — genuinely useful, but think through exactly who a distribution list reaches before turning that on for anything with pay data. Route sensitive reports to the admin first for review before forwarding, rather than blasting a full distribution list automatically. Ties directly to the salary/dollar-visibility caution in User Administration above.
- **Schedule Groups — a distinct concept from opcodes, easy to conflate (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** an opcode groups jobs for *reporting*; a **schedule group** groups people for *scheduling and printing* — a named subset of employees a manager can filter to, print, or schedule against. **Good response to the extremely common "I can't print my schedule the way I want" complaint** — before assuming a custom report is needed, check whether a schedule group solves it. **Pete's own pushback framing for this complaint, worth reusing: "We could take the entire finished schedule, print it alphabetically, and put it on the wall in the employee dining room — everyone would show up on time in the right outfit."** The system's job ends at "the numbers say you're ready for the week" — a specific printed layout is a manager preference, not an operational requirement, and there's essentially unlimited saved-view flexibility (Pete's own naming examples: "the bulletin board version," "the clipboard version," "the send-home-to-momma version") once a manager stops treating one layout as load-bearing. The **Daily Schedule Report** (one property-wide, cross-department report, readable one day at a time) doubles as a sign-in sheet and is especially useful for a manager covering staff outside their own department who don't know the team by name.

### Planning (Handbook pp. 60–76) ⭐
Weekly workflow: forecast → generate revenue centers → generate projected hours → generate/publish schedule. Teach it as a forward pipeline, then immediately pair it with the backward validation loop (actual vs. standard) from Module 1 — admins should think of planning as a loop, not a one-way process.
- **Publish as a coordinated, property-wide event, not a per-department trickle (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** managers should bring *draft* schedules to the labor meeting, not already-published ones — approve first, publish after, and publish all departments simultaneously in one coordinated window rather than staggered. The system tracks historical publish timing, which is useful for confirming compliance with whatever cadence a property adopts. Publishing itself happens at the department level with per-change notifications, which is what prevents uncommunicated edits from slipping through.
- **Mandate a timekeeping-edit cutoff before report generation, don't just suggest one (source: 08-03 PM Meeting, sharpened by Recording Highlights 02:34:27):** require the previous day's time-edit corrections to be completed each morning before Labor Effectiveness / Actual-vs-Standard reports run. At Mohonk, Pete made this a hard mandate rather than a suggestion, and the property found it **"exceedingly helpful" for identifying the actual root causes of overtime** — a demonstrated payoff worth citing, not just a data-hygiene rule. Reports run against incomplete edits don't just look slightly off, they actively obscure the overtime story admins are trying to use them to find.
- **A generalizable Housekeeping scheduling insight, worth checking at any property (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** a mismatch between shift start time and actual room availability (e.g., an 8:00 AM start when rooms don't typically clear until ~11:00 AM) creates real, avoidable idle time early in the shift. Worth checking shift start times against a property's actual checkout/availability pattern rather than assuming the traditional start time is still the right one.
- **On managing the pace of a change like that one — a useful line for setting expectations with a property's leadership (source: 08-03 PM Meeting, direct quote): "If I go too slow, the board is going to be in my face. If I go too fast, the staff is going to tar and feather me and run me out."** Cultural resistance to an operationally obvious fix is normal, not a sign the fix is wrong — the real skill is pacing the change, and it can genuinely require leadership (GM/ownership-level) support to actually land, not just an admin's recommendation.
- **Excel export gotcha, worth a heads-up before anyone builds downstream formulas:** exported reports can output numeric-looking values as text, which silently breaks any formula built on top of them until the column is explicitly converted to values. Cheap to check for, easy to miss until a formula mysteriously returns an error.
- **A best-practice publishing workflow worth offering as a named option, not a default (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** the scheduling manager marks their own schedule complete, then someone above them marks it approved — publishing can optionally be gated behind that approval step, or not, property's choice. Worth explicitly asking which mode a property is actually running in rather than assuming — it's easy for a property to end up effectively auto-approving without having deliberately chosen to.
- **Mobile-schedule terminology distinction worth teaching precisely:** a **dropped** shift means "I'm not doing this" (goes to the manager); a **given-away** shift means "I'm asking someone specific to cover it" (peer-to-peer first, manager approves after they've already agreed) — different employee intents, different workflows, easy to conflate when explaining the feature.

### Work Rules (Handbook pp. 78–83)
State law is the floor, not the answer — a property's own policy can be stricter than the state minimum, and "no state requirement" doesn't mean "no rule needed." Name every work rule to describe what it does in the title itself; the system doesn't surface this clearly elsewhere.

### Employee Maintenance (Handbook pp. 85–99) ⭐
Lower priority for a *modify-existing-standards* session specifically — cover only if data-quality issues are actively blocking a standard from calculating correctly (e.g., missing job effective dates).
- **"Field promotion" (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript)** — Pete's name for departments scheduling a brand-new hire before the ADP→Unifocus employee import has caught up ("ask forgiveness later"). A recognizable, funny-because-true framing for a very common admin support ticket — worth using live, it disarms what's otherwise a frustrating recurring complaint.
- **Why import salaried employees too, even though they're not scheduled/standard-driven:** helps with future Labor Budgeter use, and lets a labor meeting/report reveal when hourly staff are quietly covering for an absent salaried role — a useful HR-visibility side benefit, not just a budgeting one.
- **Standing recommendation, worth giving every admin day-one (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** the ADP→Unifocus import runs employees first, then hours, as two separate daily processes — either can silently fail per-employee if a job doesn't exist yet. Unifocus can send daily error emails for both ("Employee Reconcile" and "Hours Transfer") but only to whoever's subscribed — many admins don't know these exist or aren't on them. **Simple, fast support-ticket ask:** "please add [names] to the Employee Reconcile and Hours Transfer notification emails." Cheap way to catch import failures same-day instead of discovering them downstream as a missing-standard-hours mystery.
- **Two distinct error tiers on the hours-import side worth distinguishing when troubleshooting (source: 08-03 Lecture transcript):** (1) the job doesn't exist in Unifocus at all — the employee doesn't import, no labor data flows anywhere; (2) the employee and job both exist, but nothing authorizes that employee for that specific job — this one still drops hours correctly into labor reporting (so reports stay accurate) even though the error persists on the reconcile report until fixed. Knowing which tier you're looking at tells you whether reporting is actually compromised or just the error queue is noisy.
- **The "lie to it" backdating trick, for clearing an error retroactively:** hours imports carry a rolling ~21-day window, so fixing a job/authorization gap in ADP today only stops *new* errors — it doesn't retroactively clear errors already queued for the prior weeks in that window. Backdating the effective date on the corrected secondary job assignment in Unifocus (setting it earlier than today, to match when the work actually started) clears the historical error queue immediately instead of waiting for it to age out.
- **Same-day job-creation SOP, reinforced from a live example:** a property created "Fitness Desk Supervisor" in ADP and assigned an employee to it same-day, but the mirrored job was never created in Unifocus — surfaced later as a "home job must be valid" profile error. Reinforces the standing "slave to ADP" rule (§2 Labor Structure): whoever creates a job in ADP must trigger same-day creation in Unifocus, ideally via an explicit SOP naming who does the Unifocus half if it isn't the same person (many properties' HR teams don't know the Unifocus side needs to happen at all).

### User Administration (new module, source: 08-03 PM Meeting)
Security groups can behave inconsistently — a property reported "weird" environmental issues where group-based permissions didn't reliably enforce, forcing a fallback to per-user permission overrides. **Standing best practice worth giving every admin:** don't build a new user's permissions from scratch — copy an existing, working user profile with the right access level and adjust from there. Permission interdependencies get complex enough that starting from a known-good template avoids re-discovering the same gaps every time.
- **Working theory on why security groups misbehave, worth checking when this comes up elsewhere (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** group membership/removal appears to be *additive*, not managed as a clean set — removing a user from a group doesn't reliably strip the permissions that group once granted. A property that abandons groups entirely in favor of per-user permissions (defeating the point of having groups) is very likely dealing with exactly this residue, not a fundamentally broken permission system.
- **Specific, non-obvious permission interdependencies worth knowing in advance rather than discovering case by case (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** editing a Revenue Center apparently also requires Rooms Forecast edit access, even for a user who can't see Rooms KBIs at all. A user below the System Administrator level cannot move the planning date, even though nothing in the UI surfaces that dependency. The built-in permission descriptions are individually accurate but don't document *combinations* like these — treat "why can't this user do X" as a real troubleshooting category, not just a config oversight.
- **A concrete, real incident worth remembering as a known failure mode: Managed Schedules' export-to-ADP feature silently inherits whatever labor-structure security filter is active for the exporting user (source: 08-03 PM Meeting).** One property had a manager's personal filter get stuck on a narrow job subset — because the export only ran weekly, an entire week's worth of schedules went out effectively empty ("no one has a schedule, everything looks like a rainbow") before the stuck filter, not a system-wide failure, was identified as the cause. Worth checking the *exporting user's* own filter state first when an export looks incomplete, rather than assuming the interface itself is broken.
- **A specific, real-incident-backed caution: salary/dollar visibility on reports (source: 08-03 PM Meeting transcript).** If a job is marked "salary" and a user has both salary access and dollar access, reports like Labor Effectiveness can expose individual salaried employees' actual pay to anyone the report reaches. **Real example: a new admin at another property sent this report to "everybody, including salary" — real trouble followed.** The system has a setting that lets a user see dollar totals *without* seeing individual salary — verify that setting is actually configured rather than assuming it, especially before setting up any scheduled/auto-distributed report. This caution compounds directly with the Planning module's "never auto-distribute anything with salary/dollar visibility" rule below.
- **A concrete Employee Maintenance/standard-visibility gotcha, worth teaching early (source: 08-03 PM Meeting):** every manager and employee has read-only access to their own standard — they can't edit it, but they need to actually look at it, because their own Employee Maintenance choices (availability windows especially) can silently work against it. Example: a standard generating shifts starting at 8:00 AM while an employee's stated availability starts at 9:00 AM means that employee will never be eligible for those shifts — reads to the manager as "the system is broken," when it's really an availability/standard mismatch they can see for themselves if they know to check.

## 3. Modifying Existing Standards — The Audit Method

**This is the module to lead with whenever the engagement is "fix what's here," not "build from scratch"** — distinct from a green-field implementation and worth naming explicitly as its own skill.

1. **Read the shift record before touching the standard.** Does its defined length match what its own name/parenthetical claims? (Real example: a shift named for a 6.5-hour bucket but carrying 12 hours of standard — the system poured it as one 6.5-hour shift + one 5.5-hour leftover, not the two 6.5-hour shifts the name implied.)
2. **On any productivity/minutes-per-unit standard, look for the short shift first.** It's the expected artifact of the two-pass mechanic (Module 1), not proof of a bug — start troubleshooting there before assuming misconfiguration.
3. **Check the rounding threshold** is behaving as expected given the shift's minimum length.
4. **Validate against Actual vs. Standard trend over multiple weeks**, not a single day — a standard being off on one day doesn't mean it's broken; sustained variance does.
5. **If a manager is manually adding the same exception shift repeatedly, that's the signal to formalize it into the standard** rather than leaving it as a recurring manual override.
6. **Standard changes don't retroactively fix anything already generated** — editing a standard doesn't touch already-generated projected hours/schedules. The system regenerates automatically ~4x/day; a manual "Regenerate Projected Hours" tests changes immediately but risks clearing the existing schedule for that job — use cautiously, especially on a live property.
