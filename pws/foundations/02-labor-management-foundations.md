## 1. What Labor Management Actually Is

Labor management is not scheduling. It is not payroll. It is not a software system.

Labor management is the discipline of connecting business volume to staffing — systematically, transparently, and accountably — so that the right number of people are doing the right work at the right time, every day.

The software is the operating system. The consulting, the standards, and the management philosophy are what make it work.

> "It's a labor operating system. It's an accountability and transparent accountability system."
> — Pete Castellano

## 2. The Labor Management Process
*Source: Hotel_101_v2016.pptx, Slide 33*

Five stages, in sequence:

1. **Forecast** — Project business volumes (occupied rooms, covers, transactions, etc.)
2. **Plan** — Quantify labor required by position against forecasted volumes
3. **Schedule** — Build schedules that reflect the plan
4. **Report / Process** — Generate daily operating reports; compare actual vs. standard
5. **Evaluate / Control** — Identify exceptions, prompt action, instill accountability

---

## 3. Forecasting

**Features:**
- Short-term (one week) forecast of all business volumes affecting labor
- Property-specific configuration for unique volume indicators
- Multiple methodologies: regression, moving averages, calculated forecasts

**Benefits:**
- Eliminates guesswork and spreadsheet modeling
- Accurate projections → better planning and performance
- Managers edit and sign off on forecasted volumes

---

## 4. Planning

**Features:**
- Quantifies labor for all positions: fixed, variable, and staff-related
- Accommodates all work requirements by classification — shift-based, daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, or yearly
- Staffs to guest demand patterns

**Benefits:**
- Customized labor plan for all departments
- Reflects internal and external service demands by period of day (just-in-time service)
- Consistent and systematic planning process every week

---

## 5. Scheduling

**Features:**
- Accommodates individual employee training time and time-off preferences
- Priority-based scheduling: seniority, skill levels, etc.
- Accommodates multiple union contracts and job rules

**Benefits:**
- Promotes scheduling that reflects forecasted business levels
- Promotes cross-training and cross-utilization
- Improves employee satisfaction

---

## 6. Reporting & Evaluation

**Features:**
- Daily operating reports comparing actual vs. standard
- Identifies exceptions indicating cost or quality problems
- Summarizes data by week, month, or payroll period

**Benefits:**
- Highlights off-standard conditions and offers potential reasons
- Saves managers analytical time
- Instills real-time accountability

---

## 7. Labor Budgeting

**Features:**
- Annual and periodic budgets (including 30/60/90-day)
- Uses same standards as day-to-day labor management
- Incorporates end-of-month flex budget

**Benefits:**
- Saves time generating labor budgets
- Promotes budgeting that reflects agreed-to labor standards
- Reduces errors and duplication

## 8. The Gutter System
*Pete Castellano / PWS — core methodology*

### The Bowling Alley Metaphor
A labor standard is not a single number. It is a range — bounded by gutters on either side. Stay inside the gutters, and you're good. The standard lives in the lane.

### The Three Gutters

**Gutter 1 — Minimum to Be Open**
The irreducible floor. What is the minimum staffing required to unlock the doors and deliver basic service? This number does not flex with volume.

**Gutter 2 — Normal Operating Range**
The lane itself. Variable staffing that flexes with business volume. This is where the labor standard lives day-to-day.

**Gutter 3 — Stretch / Peak Standard**
High-volume conditions. The third gutter. May call for additional staffing that is only justified at true peak volume.

### The Critical Diagnostic
If they say "when it's really busy, we need another shift — it was crazy busy and service suffered," that is a legitimate stretch target.

But if you look at the data and they hit that volume 95% of the time — **it's not a stretch. It's normal.** And they are either normally overstaffed, normally understaffed, or both depending on how you look at it.

Observation and the gutters together tell you which.

## 9. The Percentile Analysis
*Pete Castellano / PWS — proprietary concept*

### The Problem
Managers use language like "dead," "slow," "busy," and "slammed." These words are not usable numbers. Neither is a flat productivity target.

### The Method
Take historical volume data (covers, rooms, transitions, etc.) and break it into percentile buckets — typically 5% units.

This allows you to say, with data:
- "You only hit that volume 5% of the time. That is a legitimate stretch."
- "You hit that volume 95% of the time. That is not a stretch — that is your normal, and your standard should reflect it."
- "You hit that volume about half the time. Let's talk about what that means for your core standard."

### Application
- Calibrates stretch targets honestly
- Defends against manager pushback with data instead of opinion
- In the Hilton context: helped predict the impact of a standard change before implementation — a small turn of the dial vs. a significant shift

### Turning Narrative Into Numbers
Ask for specific recent dates. Ask how the day went. Convert:
- "It was dead" → actual covers/rooms/transactions that day
- "We were slammed" → actual covers/rooms/transactions that day
- "It was crazy busy and service suffered" → volume threshold for stretch staffing

Now you have gutters.

## 10. The Smell Test
*Pete Castellano / PWS*

Once the gutters are set, apply the smell test:

Ask: On an average day — not the superstar, not the disaster — how many checkouts do they actually do? How many stayovers? What does a real shift look like?

If your labor standard produces a number that stays inside the gutters under those real conditions, **you're good.**

If the standard says they should do 12 stayovers in 8 hours, but they always end up borrowing from someone else or leaving work unfinished — the standard is wrong, not the people.

## 11. Housekeeping Labor Standards — The Right Approach

### The Wrong Standard
**Hours per occupied room (HPOR)** is a measurement, not a labor standard.

Problems with using HPOR as a standard:
- You do not clean occupied rooms — you clean checkouts and stayovers, which are different tasks
- Average length of stay changes throughout the year, changing the checkout/stayover mix
- A week of short-stay group business (heavy checkouts) vs. a week of long-stay leisure (heavy stayovers) produces legitimately different labor needs — but the same HPOR target
- Executive housekeepers cannot defend themselves against "you're off your standard" when the mix has shifted, because HPOR doesn't account for mix

### The Right Standard
Build separate standards for **checkouts** and **stayovers.**

Use credits (time-based units) per room type, not a blended hourly rate per occupied room.

Set those standards using:
- The gutter system (min, normal, stretch)
- The percentile analysis (how often do they actually hit each volume tier?)
- Direct observation (what actually happens in the room?)

### The Sidebar Measurement
HPOR can still be used as a **benchmarking tool** — like the sideline chains in football that measure progress in yards. Useful for comparison. Not useful for play-calling.

## 12. Standard Development Process
*Source: Hotel_101_v2016.pptx, Slide 39*

1. Review and analysis to develop standards
2. Identify fixed tasks involved
3. Identify variable tasks involved
4. Comparisons to history and budget
5. Meetings with managers
6. Approval of senior executives

*PWS note: Step 1 — "review and analysis" — is the step that has been systematically removed from most implementations. This is the gap PWS fills.*

## 15. The Why
*Pete Castellano / PWS — management philosophy*

People step forward together when they understand the why.

Most implementations tell managers *what* to do: enter your forecast, approve your schedule, look at this report. They do not explain *why* the system works, why the standard was set the way it was, or why the weekly cycle matters.

When managers understand the why:
- They trust the standard
- They defend it upward
- They use the system proactively instead of reactively
- They stop making excuses and start making adjustments
