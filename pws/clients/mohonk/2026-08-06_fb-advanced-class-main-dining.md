## 1. "I Invented the Standards" — Reframing Standards vs. Budget
- **James's frustration, stated directly:** he feels he "invented the labor standards for the system" (i.e., he was the one who originally provided the numbers), so being told a year later that a standard needs adjusting feels like being second-guessed on his own work — "no one challenged me on the labor standards when I gave them to you."
- **Pete's reframe:** the friction isn't about the labor standard itself — it's actually **Lou's separate budget/staffing-guide process** that's misaligned with it. Today those are two different things (a Unifocus labor standard and a separate budget staffing guide); **they're converging into a single unified "Labor Standard"** that will replace the separate staffing guides entirely. Not immediate, but the direction is set.
- **Coaching point on how "you're under standard" conversations land:** if a department consistently runs *under* its own standard, that's actually the easier version of the "needs adjusting" conversation — it becomes "here's a standard you're not using, let's tune it down" rather than James having to ask for *more* hours, which is always the harder ask ("the bitter pill is always more hours; cutting hours is easy"). James already runs this way voluntarily in his own budgeting — he proactively cuts hours he thinks he doesn't need rather than defending them, which is unusual manager behavior ("most people say don't ever cut").
- **Explicit reassurance: Unifocus is not being used as a cost-cutting tool.** The point of a more accurate/granular standard is accuracy, not extraction — "we're not trying to... cut it."
- **Old baseline context:** before this level of job-code granularity existed, everything ran through one undifferentiated "server" bucket — much less accurate, but much less admin overhead. The current precision is a real improvement but comes at a real time cost, which is part of what's driving James's fatigue (see §6 below).

## 2. The Deliberate Overschedule Strategy — Why, and How It's Protected
- **Confirmed again, more explicitly than in the 8/5 F&B labor meeting:** the Main Dining Room's forward-schedule overage isn't a mistake — it's intentional. James deliberately overschedules servers, then uses shift cancellations (mostly weather-driven, e.g. rained-out brunch) to trim back down to standard. Live example: "he cut 3 servers today... who wants to go home."
- **Seasonal caveat, new detail:** this only works because staff are willing to give up hours. **In winter, staff need the money and won't volunteer off** — over-scheduling has to be handled much more carefully in the off-season than in summer, when people take time off "in a heartbeat."
- **Why this is safe to do, explicitly framed by Pete:** the labor standard is James's **"shield" / "armor."** As long as the standard shows what the real labor need is, deliberately scheduling above it (and trimming via calls) can't be used against him — "if they try to take it away, then you get a sword" (i.e., the standard is what protects the practice from being read as waste).

## 3. Budget Conversation, Reframed as an Operational Conversation

- **Historical baseline example, offered directly:** last year's budget directive was flat/no-new-hours across the board regardless of what the standard math implied — with a real, unified labor standard, that kind of blind flat-cut directive becomes harder to sustain, because it now has to be reconciled against an actual operational model rather than a top-line number.
- **Performance context, this year:** tracking **under budget** despite guest volume being up and a **4% salary increase** baked in — meaning actual hours/efficiency are improving, not just holding flat. Framed as a genuinely good year so far, with the caveat "we'll see how the fall is."
- **Dollars-vs-hours principle reinforced again** (same theme as the 8/5 Rooms/CEO and F&B sessions): revenue allocation and pay-rate changes don't change how much labor a task takes — the standard is denominated in hours specifically because the work itself doesn't move when the dollars do.

## 6. Granary/Founders Rain Contingency — Covers-vs-Labor Coding Mismatch
**Long, unresolved live discussion — a genuine configuration gap, not a quick fix.**
- **The scenario:** when it rains, Granary service physically relocates indoors into the Founders space. The covers stay coded as **Granary covers**, but the servers working that relocated service are currently coded as **Founders servers** — meaning **Founders labor is being consumed with no corresponding Founders covers**, while **Granary shows covers with no matching labor**, structurally guaranteeing Founders reads as over-standard on rainy days.
- **Why it's not a simple recode:** Granary and Founders servers are paid at **different home rates** in ADP. Recoding the servers to Granary (which would fix the covers/labor mismatch) requires **manually adjusting their pay rate in the payroll report** for those shifts — real extra administrative work, not just a Unifocus field change.
- **Working conclusion, not fully finalized:** James's leaning read is that the least-bad option is to **code the relocated servers as Granary servers** (matching the covers) and eat the manual ADP pay-rate correction — "that's how I'll do it." Explicitly framed as James's call to make, not Lou's or Pete's ("Lou's got bigger fish to fry").
- **General principle reaffirmed, consistent with the rest of this KB:** covers and labor coding need to match to the same bucket — you can't get credit for covers in one department while charging the labor to another; "the labor should match wherever you put the covers."
- **Tied to the standing schedule-to-ADP integration project:** this whole class of problem (home rate, home department) is explicitly called out as one of the reasons the **future "push schedules into ADP" project** matters — without the schedule itself flowing into ADP, staff default to their home department/rate for everything, which is part of what's forcing this manual workaround in the first place.

## 8. The Overschedule-Then-Trim Mechanic, Explained in Full Detail
- **The actual method, described step by step for the first time in this KB:** James deliberately schedules his highest-hour servers (currently ~41–44 hrs before any calls) onto the shifts **most likely to get cancelled** — brunch is the top candidate (weather-dependent), dinner the least likely (people rarely no-show dinner). He works down the list from highest-hours to lowest as he assigns on-call/most-likely-off shifts, so that if/when a shift does get cancelled, it's disproportionately taken from someone who was already running hot.
- **Explicit acknowledgment the standard itself runs a little generous:** shift end times are set "in the middle" (e.g., 10:30 PM) to cover the range of when people actually tend to leave (9:30–10:30 PM) — meaning most actual shifts run a bit shorter than the standard assumes, which is by design, not an error: "it's gonna average out."
- **Result, current state:** overtime is down to roughly 20–25 hours over the whole department during the **busiest week of the summer**, described by both as a strong outcome.
- **Live system walkthrough:** demoed the schedule editor's Utilization sort (high-to-low) to visually identify the highest-hour employees, and the mechanism for tagging on-call shifts with a note (label, not a system field) that counts toward total scheduled hours but visually flags who's on the "most likely to be sent home" list.
- **Small naming gap flagged:** the "Server" job code for Main Dining Room doesn't carry an outlet prefix the way other jobs do (e.g., "MDR Server" vs. plain "Server") — worth a naming cleanup so it's visually distinguishable from Banquet/Wedding/Founders servers at a glance. Not fixed live.

## 9. Technical Gap Found: Partial-Shift Generation on Run-Rate Standards
**⚠️ New, real limitation — worth tracking as its own item.**
- **The problem, as James described it:** when Generate Schedules builds shifts off a run-rate/ratio-based standard (e.g., "1 server per 25 covers"), it can produce a **partial shift** rather than a full standard-length shift — e.g., someone clocked in at 5:15 PM but cut off at 8:30 PM instead of the normal 10:30 PM end time, because the ratio math only justified ~4 hours of coverage rather than a full shift.
- **James has been asking for full-shift enforcement "for about a year"** and been told repeatedly that it isn't something that could be done for him.
- **Real, concrete risk this creates:** managers sometimes copy/paste an existing full-length shift over a generated partial shift to "fix" it visually, without necessarily tracking that they've just added real hours — James's own worked example: four dinner shifts that were each ~2 hours short, silently copy-pasted to full length, would turn a 42-hour week into 48 hours without anyone deliberately deciding to add 6 hours.
- **Pete's diagnosis, stated directly and clearly:** this is a structural tension between two different kinds of standard — a **scheduling standard** (which can enforce fixed shift lengths) and a **run-rate standard** (which computes headcount from a ratio and has no inherent concept of a "whole shift," so it can't guarantee it won't shave a partial one off the end). "It can't take everybody and shave a couple minutes off it... it's making a minimum shift length." **Not promised as fixable** — Pete said he'd look at it, not that there's a solution in hand.
- **No resolution reached live.** Worth flagging to Susanna/Monali as a real methodology question (can a run-rate-based standard be configured to only ever generate whole shifts?), separate from any specific Mohonk bug.
