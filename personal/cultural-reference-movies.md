# Cultural Reference — Movies, TV & Commercials

Media that holds important cultural references that transcend the source material.
Started as a list Pete made his kids watch — expanded from a work project where experienced consultants would quote movies and newer colleagues had no idea what they were talking about.

At a Unifocus in-person conference, the team spent a month building a list and voted March Madness-style on which movie to watch together. **Young Frankenstein** and **Airplane** won.

**On parodies:** Parodies are important because you HAVE to understand — or are forced to learn — the underlying truth of why they are funny. They teach the source material by demanding it.

## Movies

| Movie | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| The Wizard of Oz (1939) | "There's no place like home"; "Pay no attention to the man behind the curtain"; the yellow brick road; Technicolor as a narrative device; cultural shorthand for transformation, illusion, and the power of home |
| The Godfather (franchise) | "I'm gonna make him an offer he can't refuse"; the definitive American crime saga; power, loyalty, and family as moral framework; widely considered among the greatest films ever made |
| Ghostbusters (1984) | "Who you gonna call?"; "Don't cross the streams"; blue-collar entrepreneurship vs. government bureaucracy; defined 1980s blockbuster comedy |
| The Blues Brothers (1980) | "We're on a mission from God"; living history of soul, R&B, and Chicago blues (Aretha Franklin, Ray Charles, James Brown); music as higher calling |
| Animal House (1978) | Defined the college comedy genre; "toga party" entered the lexicon; Belushi as id unleashed; rebellion against institutional authority; age-appropriate before going to college |
| Legally Blonde (2001) | Female competence dismissed and then vindicated; "bend and snap"; overturning assumptions about intelligence and ambition; more subversive than it appears |
| This Is Spinal Tap (1984) | Invented the mockumentary format; "Turn it up to 11" — exceeding the assumed maximum scale; taught a generation how to skewer self-importance |
| Mr. Mom (1983) | "220, 221 — whatever it takes" — confident improvisation; role reversal comedy; challenged gender norms in mainstream film; Michael Keaton's breakthrough |
| Young Frankenstein (1974) | Mel Brooks parody of classic Universal horror; "Walk this way"; "Put the candle back"; teaches the original Frankenstein by demanding you understand what's being mocked; won Unifocus conference vote |
| Airplane! (1980) | "Don't call me Shirley"; redefined comedic density (jokes per minute); deadpan absurdism; parody of 1970s disaster films; still the benchmark for spoof comedy; won Unifocus conference vote |
| Austin Powers: International Man of Mystery (1997) | "Yeah, baby!"; parody of James Bond and 1960s spy culture; "shagadelic"; introduced a generation to the tropes it was lampooning |
| The Naked Gun (1988) | Leslie Nielsen's deadpan as an art form; slapstick elevated to absurdism; origin in the short-lived Police Squad! TV series; "Nothing to see here" |
| Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975) | "It's just a flesh wound"; "We are the knights who say Ni!"; absurdist British humor as high art; low-budget constraints became creative tools; foundational for a certain style of ironic comedy |
| Ferris Bueller's Day Off (1986) | "Life moves pretty fast. If you don't stop and look around once in a while, you could miss it"; "Bueller… Bueller…"; Gen X manifesto; breaking the fourth wall as storytelling device |
| Caddyshack (1980) | "Be the ball"; "Cinderella story"; class warfare at the country club; Bill Murray, Chevy Chase, Rodney Dangerfield; defined golf comedy and much of early-80s ensemble humor |
| Spaceballs (1987) | Mel Brooks Star Wars parody; "May the Schwartz be with you"; "Ludicrous speed"; "Merchandising, merchandising, merchandising" — the first major film to mock its own franchise economics |
| Blazing Saddles (1974) | Mel Brooks uses the western genre to dissect American racism; breaks the fourth wall literally; "Badges? We don't need no stinking badges" (parodying Sierra Madre); pushes the boundary of what comedy is allowed to say |
| Monty Python's The Meaning of Life (1983) | Satirizes every stage of human existence — birth, war, religion, death; "Every sperm is sacred"; Mr. Creosote; darkly absurdist where Holy Grail is whimsical |
| Monty Python's Life of Brian (1979) | "He's not the Messiah — he's a very naughty boy!"; "Always look on the bright side of life"; religious satire banned in several countries; companion piece to Holy Grail but sharper and more pointed |
| The Treasure of the Sierra Madre (1948) | Original source of "We don't need no stinking badges"; Bogart against type; greed, paranoia, and the corrupting power of wealth; one of the great American morality films |
| The Princess Bride (1987) | "As you wish"; "Inconceivable!"; "Hello. My name is Inigo Montoya. You killed my father. Prepare to die."; fairy tale subversion; cult classic that rewards every rewatch |
| It's a Wonderful Life (1946) | "Every time a bell rings an angel gets his wings"; "No man is a failure who has friends"; the definitive American film about the value of an ordinary life; Christmas cultural bedrock |
| Soylent Green (1973) | "Soylent Green is people!"; dystopian vision of overpopulation, environmental collapse, and corporate food control; Charlton Heston; one of cinema's great twist endings |
| Planet of the Apes (1968) | "Take your stinking paws off me, you damned dirty ape!"; the Statue of Liberty ending — one of film's most iconic twists; nuclear war allegory; social commentary on race, evolution, and human arrogance. Original only. |
| Shane (1953) | "Shane, come back!"; quintessential American western moral framework — the reluctant hero who cannot escape his past; a man of violence choosing peace; influenced nearly every western and action film that followed |
| The Searchers (1956) | Pete: best John Wayne film. Widely considered the greatest American western ever made; obsession, racism, and the dark underside of frontier mythology; "That'll be the day"; direct influence on Scorsese, Spielberg, Lucas, and Ford's own legacy |
| E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982) | "E.T. phone home"; the bicycle silhouette against the moon; Spielberg at his most purely emotional; childhood wonder, friendship, and loss; the alien as innocent outsider; John Williams score is inseparable from the imagery |

## TV Shows

| Show | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Get Smart (1965–1970) | Maxwell Smart = Agent 86. Spy parody — only works if you know spy tropes. Pete pondering "Agent 86" as the name for his Chief of Staff agent. |
| The Brady Bunch (1969–1974) | Cultural shorthand for blended family, suburban idealism, American TV family mythology |
| Gilligan's Island (1964–1967) | Every character is an archetype; the situation is a lens for human nature |
