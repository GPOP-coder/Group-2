## Core Belief

Photography is the connection to many of Pete's thoughts. It is not a hobby — it is how he connects his thoughts, anchors his memories, and processes his world. A picture is worth a thousand words. Pete has hundreds of thousands of pictures.

His images are an external memory system, a creative lens, and a thread running through many of his other beliefs and projects. The book concepts (Kim's story, the BTTR bridge book) are both deeply visual. His eBay work is rooted in seeing value others miss. His interpersonal superpower is reading people. All of it has a photographer's eye underneath it.

## Time and the Slice

Every photograph is a fraction of a second. Add up every exposure Pete has ever made and the total is a tiny sliver of a life. Time cannot be stopped, bottled, or saved — ref: Jim Croce, "Time in a Bottle." A photograph doesn't capture time. It takes a slice of it. That slice becomes the anchor. The rest flows on.

This is part of why photography matters to Pete — not to stop time, but to acknowledge it was passing, and that this moment was worth noticing.

This connects to the gratitude framework: the radical acceptance of the present — that's what the shutter does. It says: right now, this. Not before, not after. Just this fraction of a second.

## Truth, Distortion, and the Eye

Pete's father was a Naval intelligence photo interpreter whose creed was "the camera never lies." Pete absorbed this as gospel, then spent years discovering how perspective, exposure, and processing alter reality. AI manipulation is the latest chapter in a very old story.

Pete's father never taught him photography. Pete found it himself in a 5th/6th grade class that produced the school yearbook — rolling his own black and white film, shooting manual 35mm, developing his own prints. Trial and error, no inherited technique.

**Pete's aesthetic is photo realism.** He uses HDR not to distort but to compensate — combining low, medium, and high exposures to approximate what the human eye actually sees (the eye processes 3–4x the f-stop range of the best camera). Cartoonish HDR stretches past reality; Pete's HDR tries to reach it. He knows where the boundary is, which means crossing it is always a choice, never an accident.

He also shoots fisheye. Takes straight lines and twists them. Plays with distortion and exaggeration deliberately.

Pete's own observation: *"Maybe that has less to do with photography and more about my outlook. Maybe."*

The photographer chooses the lens.

## How Pete Found Photography

**Age 15 — Sears Portrait Studio (first job):** Shooting baby and child portraits on a prescribed formula. Each age group had a sequence: this shot, then that, this background, then that. He learned technique, but the more durable lesson was commercial: if you printed every prescribed shot and laid them all in front of Mom, it was almost impossible for her not to buy them all. The ones she didn't buy right now would be discarded. Urgency without pressure. The product sold itself.

**High school:** While other students shot school plays, musicals, and marching band for class, Pete bought his own camera and sold the pictures to parents. He paid for all his camera equipment in high school that way.

**Parenthood:** Shot his own daughters (Jessica and Jennie) in dance class and marching band — and sold pictures of the other kids to their parents. Same model, same instinct.

The through-line: Pete has always seen photography as both art and exchange. The picture has value. The parent can't say no. And the photographer funds his own next camera.
