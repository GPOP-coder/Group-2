## CORE INSIGHT

The value of items is in using them, not keeping them.

---

## THE PLATES — HAPPY HOUR SPREADS TO THE WORLD

Dad hosted his own Monday Happy Hours the last few years he was in the house. The plates from those gatherings were packed, assessed, and donated — 3 boxes of plates, cups, glasses, vases — to the Redemption Store at Perdido Bay Church.

The memory isn't in the plate. The memory is already inside me.

By letting the plates go, I'm not losing Happy Hour. I'm spreading it. Whoever gets those plates — however they use them, wherever they go — will carry Happy Hour with them. They don't have to know. I will know. The items may exude the happiness of those memories and pass them forward to new owners. Or they will absorb and carry entirely new memories. Or they will be broken or discarded. It doesn't matter. The memories are no longer attached to the plates. They never had to be.

## PHOTOGRAPHY AS MEMORY STORAGE

Photography is a better way to store and retrieve memories than holding on to stuff. A photograph captures the moment without requiring you to keep the object. The object can move on. The memory stays.

## THE SINGLE SOCK PARADOX — TOGETHERNESS IS VALUE

Socks are born together. Bought together, put on together, worn together, removed together. They should stay together through wash, dry, fold, put away. When you look for them again, they should always be together. They should even be thrown away together.

A single sock has less value. At minimum, you waste time finding the other one. At worst, you waste time and never find it — and then the time is gone AND the remaining sock is worthless. What do you do with it? Put it back in the drawer? You'll do the same thing all over again. Throw it away? Then you find the other one later, and now you have one discarded and one waiting. Both useless. When one gets worn out, it's best to throw them both out. The only reasonable move is to isolate it, save it, and match the batch later.

The solution was simple: **pin your socks together when you take them off.** Not a request. A mandate — not for power or control. Because it was a good idea, for good reasons. Together, socks are always ready. If you lose them, you lose them together. So what.

---

**The day it started.** Kim was working at the Disney Store while she was still teaching — before her master's, before Jennifer. She'd buy the girls complete outfits: dress or shirt and pants, hat, shoes, socks — all matching, all perfect. One morning, running late for school, a missing sock generated a meltdown. Heat, anger, frustration, tears — over a sock. I pledged it would never happen again.

Kim and the girls thought the sock-pinning idea was silly. The more I insisted, the more they resisted. We found novelty single-sock stores for kids in Kansas City, Orlando, maybe another city. Cute, unique, funny — and a way to mock me. Which was also funny, in its truth. At least they made the connection. But they were obstinate. Not a maybe or someday. Just no. I kept a separate bin in the laundry room for the singles, convinced that one day we'd have a big sock-sorting party and find all the matches. Nope.

---

**The sorting.** Recently I was cleaning out the basement. I found the bins — years of single socks. I announced the project, thinking Kim would play along. I even had prizes ready for whoever found the most matches. She sat in her recliner and watched me dump them all on the floor. Didn't move.

Every match I found, I was relieved. *See! Another one.* No reaction. I kept finding them and exaggerating my excitement. Kim FaceTimed the girls to show them what dad was doing. I got some acknowledgment, some jabs. *Look what dad's doing. Isn't that fun and funny.*

It actually upset me. I started overreacting to each match. And then saying it out loud — every match was another example. Proof. Another time a simple request was ignored, mocked, disregarded. *Look, another one.* One more time I was invisible. Non-existent. Irrelevant. Deliberately disobeyed.

Kim thought I was joking. I wasn't.

I found over 100 matches. Each one was proof that what I'd felt for over 30 years was valid and real. They were a physical manifestation: *I don't matter.*

---

**And yet.** Pinning your socks together is still a good idea. I still do it.

---

**The resolution.** The 100+ matches didn't go to waste. Some still fit me or Kim — back in circulation. Some the grandkids are wearing now. Some got donated. A small collection remains, waiting on a match. Because you don't throw away what might still find its other half.

The grand prize for my imaginary family sock-sorting party — the prize nobody won because nobody played — was a wall sculpture of giant safety pins. I bought it anyway.

At the bottom of the bin, I found two little girl Disney socks. Different patterns. One of them — I don't know which — is the culprit. The one that started it all. The original missing sock. But here's the thing: I still don't know if the other one was thrown away years ago and this is the one that finally surfaced. Or if this was the first single sock ever put in the bin, and its match is still out there somewhere, waiting to be found.

That is the mystery and the conundrum behind every single sock.

*Which one am I?* it wonders. The lost one that finally came home? Or the one still waiting?

The two Disney socks are on display in the laundry room now. Draped over two of the giant safety pins. A monument to the argument. Visible to everyone doing laundry. Every day.

I won.

*This belongs in the book.*

## THE RED COUCH — ANOTHER SINGLE SOCK

The couch was donated to Teen Challenge on Wednesday, May 20, 2026. Cleared space in storage. Cleared space inside.

But one cushion got separated in the move. Left behind in storage while the couch went ahead without it. A couch missing a cushion isn't really a couch. It's diminished. Incomplete. Worth less — maybe worthless — to whoever receives it.

The next morning I drove the cushion to the Teen Challenge store. The guys at the counter already knew: there was a red couch in the back, missing a cushion. They went to the back room. They came out with one.

It matched.

The relief was real and disproportionate. Because it wasn't about the cushion. It was the same feeling as finding a sock match — the same OCD-adjacent satisfaction of wholeness restored. Two things that belong together, together again. The couch is complete. It has full value now. Someone will buy it. It will go somewhere and be used.

The cushion was a single sock. It just happened to be upholstered.

## PRICE IS NOT VALUE

A Brother laser printer sells at auction for $33.90. It works. It has Genuine toner installed. It's worth $150 used on a fair day, $250 new. It sells for $33.90.

Why?

The seller isn't wrong. They're not confused about the market. They're optimizing for something other than the item's value — space, time, urgency, or the simple fact that they already have a replacement and this machine has served its purpose. Waiting would cost them more than the gap between $33.90 and $150. So they price for their situation, not the item's worth.

Sometimes people don't know the value of what they have. Or the situation lowers the value. They just need it gone. If they had more time and exposure to more buyers, they would get more money. Waiting may have a higher cost to them. Or they already have a replacement and this model has served its purpose.

**Not all prices are tied to the value of the item.**

The buyer who knows the value and has the patience to watch for exactly this moment captures that gap. Not by exploiting anyone — the seller got what they needed. The buyer got what they waited for. The transaction is fair on both sides, even though the price looks wrong on the surface.

On the buying side, knowing the value and having the patience to watch for a situation like this is value added.

This plays out everywhere: a relationship that ends not because it lost its value but because the timing or circumstances made staying too costly. A house that sells below market because the seller needs it gone more than they need the extra $20,000. A piece of advice, freely given, that lands at exactly the moment someone is ready to hear it — and would have meant nothing a year earlier.

Value is real. Price is situational. The two only occasionally align.
