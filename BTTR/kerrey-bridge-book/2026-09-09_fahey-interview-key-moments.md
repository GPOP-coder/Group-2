# Mike Fahey Interview — Key Moments & Quotes

- **Conducted by:** John Dechant, Jim Fogarty (Legacy videography team)
- **Also present:** Larry Foster (Director of Parks & Recreation, project organizer)

## Where Fahey Fits in the Story

Kerrey originated the riverfront vision and secured the initial $18M in federal funding (1999). Daub prioritized the riverfront and set up the funding pathway (Alternative Pathways Trust Fund) that made the bridge possible. **Fahey inherited the actual bridge project** when he took office — including a bid process that had already gone out under Daub — and is the mayor who lived through the crisis moment, drove the redesign, closed the funding gap, and cut the ribbon when it opened.

## Key Facts Confirmed On the Record

### The $44 Million Bid — The Crisis Moment
- The original bid (RFP process started under Hal Daub) came back at **$44 million** — roughly double what was expected. Fahey: *"Sticker shock was all over all of us."*
- Bids were opened in **Lincoln**; Larry Foster and FIGG's engineer Alan Phipps drove down together and got the news live, with press finding out almost immediately.
- Fahey's own account of that day: *"I don't think I had a mind that day."* His only real feedback in the moment was from Larry Foster — there was no outside advisory process, no alternatives considered besides redesign.
- Fahey never seriously considered walking away: *"I wasn't going to leave $18 million on the table that we are earmarked for. I mean, that would've been stupid."*

### The Redesign That Cut the Cost in Half
- Original design (already cable-stayed) was reworked — Fahey recalls the change coming down to the cable/structural approach, though he wasn't sure of the technical specifics ("I don't know what changed that").
- Redesign started **2004**; construction began **October 2006**; bridge opened **October 28, 2008** — about two years of construction.
- Final cost: **~$22-23 million — half the $44M bad bid.**
- Design firm: **HNTB** (same firm that produced the original cable-stayed concept image years earlier for the Back to the River study). **FIGG** was the firm behind the unsuccessful/expensive original design. Design-build competition was then run and **HNTB won**. **APAC** (a Kansas company) was the actual construction contractor.

### Closing the Funding Gap
- After redesign, still a shortfall of roughly **$3-4 million** against the ~$18-19M federal money already secured.
- Sources that closed it: City of Omaha and Council Bluffs both contributed (Omaha put in more — Fahey recalls **~$2-2.5 million** from Omaha); Back to the River raised money directly; some private money.
- **Kiewit Foundation** contributed **$1 million** — Larry Foster brought this in, with a specific condition from Lyn Ziegenbein: Kiewit would participate only if **Iowa West Foundation** also participated on the Iowa side.
- **Iowa West Foundation** came through — Larry set up a meeting at the Hilton with then-Executive Director **Todd Graham**, Fahey attended. **John Nelson**, a personal friend of Fahey's on Iowa West's board, was "the bird dog" who helped get it approved.
- Fahey raised property taxes slightly in his first budget (**~0.5-0.6%**) partly to help fund the city's share — caught real criticism for it, but Daub had been planning to raise taxes too and Fahey ended up doing it instead: *"Hal was planning on raising taxes, so he dodged that bullet."*
- **Carol Ebdon** was Fahey's Finance Director for his entire term (successor to **Stan Timm**, who served under Daub) — she was Fahey's key partner working out how to close the gap.

### City Council Votes — Not Unanimous
- Omaha City Council approved on a **4-3** vote (Republican-majority council at the time; Fahey recalls Chuck, Dan, Kraft [D], Jim Vokal as members involved — "Democrats were still somewhat leading it").
- Council Bluffs' city council also had to approve, and also did.
- Despite multiple layers of government (two cities, two states, federal), Fahey recalls **no major fights** over approvals: *"It was a very collaborative effort."* Larry Foster's read: once federal officials saw the project and understood the Bob Kerrey connection, "they were inclined to help rather than hinder."

### Naming the Bridge
- Fahey does not claim credit for naming it after Kerrey, despite some people assuming it was his idea: *"I thought it was appropriate... I don't think I pushed it... I always thought it was a collaborative effort."* Believes it may have originated with Back to the River, but nobody interviewed so far seems to know for certain.
- No real controversy over the name, per Fahey and Larry Foster both.

### Public Pushback (Context, Not New)
- Newspaper "public pulse" criticism of spending the $18M federal money this way was still ongoing when Fahey took office (had started under Daub) — in the same category as the streetcar and the ballpark: *"Intuitively, you know it's the right thing to do, but..."* not something you'd want to put to a public vote.

### The Lighting Issue
- A real, if secondary, construction-era problem: a dispute/ambiguity over whether lighting was included in the original specs — required direct conversation with HNTB to resolve. Larry Foster: *"They were damn big [at the time]... What do you mean there's no lights on the bridge?"*

### Legacy and Comparison
- Fahey ranks the bridge **"right there with the ballpark"** as his proudest legacy achievements as mayor.
- He was still in office and personally cut the ribbon at the bridge opening (unlike the ballpark, where Suttle — his successor — cut the ribbon after Fahey had left office).
- Direct St. Louis Arch comparison, made unprompted: *"St. Louis has got their arch, we've got our bridge."*
- Believes it may have been **the only pedestrian bridge connecting two states** at the time it opened ("It was at the time," per Larry Foster).
- Alternatives to a bridge were seriously studied earlier in the project's life: a water taxi and a gondola system were both considered and ruled out as impractical (Larry Foster was the gondola advocate — killed partly by the "how do you get five bicycles on a gondola" problem).

### Timeline Fahey Volunteered Unprompted (useful for the book's chronology)
- **1998** — Bob Kerrey forms the river vision.
- **1999** — Kerrey secures the $18M via a federal transportation bill.
- **2001-2003** — Mostly Larry Foster and the mayor's office working on funding support and design planning.
- **2004** — Redesign begins (after the bad bid).
- **Oct 2006** — Construction begins.
- **Oct 28, 2008** — Bridge opens.
- Fahey is not certain whether the original bid that came back at $44M went out under Daub or under his own administration — "It started under Hal," per Larry Foster, which Fahey accepted.

## Color / Personal Anecdotes (light touch, book-flavor material)

- **The $40 parking spot:** The day after leaving office, during the College World Series, Fahey — no longer entitled to his mayoral parking spot — paid a stranger to park in his driveway. Recognized as the former mayor, he was charged **$40 instead of the going $20 rate**. *"I didn't want to get my tire sliced."*
- **The College World Series attendance-guessing tradition:** Larry Foster describes a running gag with season-ticket holder Greg Peterson, using pre-computerized ticket-count data to predict announced attendance figures almost exactly — Peterson became a local "number guru" and nobody could figure out how.
- **The "Mayor Fahey, bite me" sign:** During the contentious Elkhorn annexation fight, a liquor store near 204th put up a sign reading "Mayor Fahey, bite me." Fahey wanted a photo with it but never dared. Also refused to let staff tear down a disputed water tower out there after the annexation was won.
- **Rick's Café Boatyard:** Fahey denies (despite rumor) ever having ownership in the riverfront restaurant, unlike some others of the era; recalls it fondly as "before its time" for downtown Omaha.
- **Tom Hanafan relationship:** Daily interaction during the project; genuinely close, decades-long friendship (Hanafan reportedly told Fahey he was "like the 20th" Omaha mayor he'd trained). Fahey knew him personally well before becoming mayor, through a family connection (Hanafan was a friend of Fahey's brother).
