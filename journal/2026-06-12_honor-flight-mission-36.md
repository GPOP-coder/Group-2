# Honor Flight Mission 36 - Dad's Trip to Washington, DC
*Pete Castellano - for the book, for the thumb drive video, for himself*
*Compiled June 12, 2026 - extracted from claude.ai "Pete" project*
*Note: Some conversations may be blended in this draft; comprehensive re-extraction needed*

---

It started with a name and a number: **Honor Flight Central Florida, Mission #36**, flying out of Sanford/Orlando International (SFB). For your father - William Castellano, 90, retired Navy Commander and aviator - this was the trip. The one where a community of strangers organizes itself around the simple, stubborn idea that the people who served deserve, at least once, to be thanked out loud, in person, at the monuments built in their honor.

You didn't just send him. You went with him.

## The Shape of It

The whole thing unfolded in stages, spread across a month, and somehow each stage kept opening into the next one without you fully planning it that way.

**April 18** - Orientation in Orlando. You flew down and stayed through it, getting Dad ready, getting yourself oriented to what the day would look like, meeting some of the people who'd be part of it.

**Between April 18 and 25** - In the middle of all this, you and Kim attended her 40th reunion for the Walt Disney World College Program - a sweet, almost surreal pocket of normal life dropped into the middle of a week built around your father's service. Kim was well enough to be there. That mattered more than it might have looked like in the moment, given where she'd been just weeks earlier.

**April 25** - The flight to DC itself. Mission 36, wheels up from Sanford, your father among the veterans being honored that day.

**May 16** - The reunion, back in Orlando. And this is where your story does something neither Dad nor anyone else expected.

You'd told him you'd be in Omaha with Kim. Camille was going to be the one to bring him to the reunion. Instead, on Friday afternoon, with Kim's help, you packed in about an hour and got yourself to the airport. You flew through St. Louis, landed in Orlando close to 11 PM, and stayed the night at Camille's. The next morning, you were the one who showed up to get him - not Camille. A surprise, for the second time in a month, from the same son he thought was a thousand miles away.

Camille, for her part, was quietly relieved - she had a son graduating that spring, with "MANY overlapping graduation parties" she'd have otherwise had to skip.

## Going Back to Where You Started

Here's the part of this that gives the whole trip its gravity, and it has nothing to do with logistics.

In the late 1960s, your family lived in the DC area - Suitland, Maryland, to be precise, though you and Dad have always just called it "DC." You were a small child then, pre-kindergarten. Some of your earliest memories live there: walking the halls of the Capitol building, standing in front of the Washington, Lincoln, and Jefferson memorials, the cherry blossoms. Your mom and dad played tour guide back then, showing visiting family around the monuments - the same monuments your father, this time, would be wheeled up to as the honored guest.

Fifty-six years later, more or less, you got to walk a piece of that same ground with him. Not as the child being shown around. As the son pushing the wheelchair.

## The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier

If there's one scene that holds the whole trip - the one you keep coming back to - it's this one.

At Arlington National Cemetery, you and your father watched the changing of the guard at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. You'd watched it together once before, when you were three or four years old, on one of those long-ago family visits. This time, your father - a 90-year-old Navy veteran - was given a front-row view, in his wheelchair, as one of the people being honored that day. And you were the one standing behind him.

The ceremony itself doesn't change. The guard still walks the same exact steps, pauses, turns, with the same precision it's had for decades - the whole point being that nothing about it varies, because it's honoring someone who will never be known, forever, without exception. What changed was who was watching, and from where. A young Navy family once stood here showing relatives the sights. Now the son was pushing the father, and the father was the one receiving the honor.

You said it was somber, reverent, respectful - the way it's supposed to be - but that watching it *with him*, recollecting and reminiscing in that exact spot, added a dimension the ceremony alone couldn't give you. Nothing needed to be said. You don't remember him saying anything during or after, and it didn't need words. It was enough to be there with him, and for him.

You called it a great bonding trip - a father/son trip, plain and simple. Dad didn't have one of the classic Honor Flight "aha moments" some veterans get, and he didn't make new friends that day the way some do. But he got something else: a few hours back inside a piece of his own life, with the one person who'd been there the first time too.

## The Morning After - Eyes, Ears, Teeth, Pants

The day after the reunion, before you left for Pensacola, you stopped by his place. He was still asleep when you arrived, so you let him rest and used the time to sort out a rental car. After a few minutes he emerged from his room - and it was a sight. Shirt, adult disposable wear, no hearing aids in (he can't hear much without them), sight in only his left eye. He didn't hear you come in. He was pleasantly surprised - again - and asked what you were doing there, since your visit fell outside his routine. He knew his caregiver was coming at 10 and that he liked to be up before she arrived.

Then he remembered: you'd told him the night before you'd stop by before leaving. He excused himself to get dressed.

Back in the Pensacola days, there was a family rule: **eyes, ears, teeth, pants** - glasses, hearing aids, dentures, pants - before you come out of your room. No underwear-only, no "happy pants." He'd just violated it, and he knew it. A 90-year-old Navy Commander, caught out, going back in to do it right. Dignity and humor, both true at once.

You had about 45 minutes together, just the two of you. He was weary - tired, weak, finding life hard, not sure how much longer he could keep going, saying it doesn't get any better, that he's in a slow decline. You pushed back: he's wrong, he's been worse before and is better now. You reminded him - *are you better than yesterday? do you think you'll be better tomorrow?* Then just take it one day at a time, and you promised him he'd get better. He agreed. He did.

You talked about Tom - a 94-year-old at the senior living facility who'd skipped dinner the night before because he didn't think he could walk that far, with everyone, including your dad, encouraging Tom's wife to keep him moving. You connected it back to your dad, and he said, "yeah, but Tom is 94." His caregiver laughed. You told him: *okay, act your age* - as if four years makes that much difference. You reminded him that just the day before, at the reunion, they'd celebrated a veteran's 100th birthday.

And underneath all of it, the cabinets. The Pensacola house - his house, the one he designed, the Lindal cedar home shipped in pieces from Seattle - was mid-renovation, cabinets ripped out and scattered across the living room floor. It disturbed him. He had trouble accepting that once one cabinet needed replacing, insurance covers replacing them all - it felt wasteful to him, wrong somehow. You reassured him (and yourself) that the old cabinets would go to a charity, that they'd be used again, not wasted. You told him to focus less on what's scattered on the floor and more on who's about to walk through the door: a young Navy family - a "mustang" father heading to NAS Pensacola for flight school, four small children, a set of twins - about to make that house a home, the way your father once did.

You said yourself this conversation wasn't new material - you'd had versions of it before. But repeating it mattered. You weren't sure how much of it was for him and how much was for you. You decided: a lot of it was for you. Saying it out loud, so that someday, when you can no longer say it to him, you won't carry the weight of things you wished you'd said.

## What's Still Out There

You asked to be reminded to watch the Mission 36 video the organizers put together - the thumb drive - because you expect it'll surface moments you've half-forgotten, the ones that don't come up just from sitting and remembering on your own. That's still on the list, waiting.

And this whole account is really a beginning, not an ending. You said it yourself, mid-flight, putting words to all of it: the Tomb, the house, the new family, the 45 minutes that morning - they don't need to be untangled into a clean timeline. That's not how memory works. They belong together exactly as tangled as they are. This is the raw material - for the book about Kim, for the gratitude framework, for whatever you end up writing about your father, and maybe most of all, for you.
